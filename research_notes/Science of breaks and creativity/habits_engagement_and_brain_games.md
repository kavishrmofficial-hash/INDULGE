# Habits, engagement mechanics, and brain games: evidence-graded notes for a gamified "Break" section (12-person creative agency)

Scope note: Findings below come from web search result summaries of the named papers; most publisher sites (JAMA, ScienceDirect, OUP, PLOS, PubMed/PMC) were blocked by the session's egress proxy, so primary-text verification was not possible for several numbers. Where a number is reported here it appeared in the search summary of the named source; where I could not confirm a number it is listed under Gaps rather than stated. Grades used throughout: **Replicated** (multiple independent studies or meta-analysis), **Single-study**, **Mixed**, **Backfires**.

## Q1. Habit formation: how long, which cues, consistency, habit stacking

### Takeaway
Habit automaticity in Lally's field data plateaued after a median of 66 days with a huge individual range (18-254), so a product should plan for a 2-3 month on-ramp, not 21 days. The strongest evidence is for event-based cues anchored to an existing routine (Judah 2013; Gollwitzer and Sheeran's implementation-intention meta-analysis, d = 0.65); "habit stacking" as popularised by Fogg and Clear is consistent with that literature but has little peer-reviewed evidence of its own, and reminders alone can support repetition while hindering automaticity.

### Cited Findings
- Lally et al. 2010 (UCL): 96 participants chose one new daily eating/drinking/activity behaviour and self-reported automaticity daily for 84 days; asymptotic curves fitted per person showed a median of 66 days to plateau, range 18 to 254 days; the paper's point is that there is no single number — [Surrey/Lally interview](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally); [Lally et al. 2010, Eur J Soc Psychol](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/32898894_How_are_habits_formed_Modeling_habit_formation_in_the_real_world)
- Lally's analysis showed missing a single day did not materially reduce the chance of forming the habit (secondary summaries of the 2010 paper) — [Steps.org summary of Lally 2010](https://steps.org/sources/lally-2010-habit-formation/)
- Judah, Gardner and Aunger 2013 (flossing, n = 50): participants told to floss *after* brushing formed stronger habits than those told to floss *before*; at 8-month follow-up the "after" group had stronger automaticity and flossed more often; authors argue the end of an existing routine is a better anchor than the middle — [Judah et al. 2013, Br J Health Psychol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22989272/)
- Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006 meta-analysis of implementation intentions ("if situation X, then I do Y"): 94 independent tests, >8,000 participants, d = 0.65 on goal attainment versus intentions alone — [Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006 summary](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-gollwitzer-how-to/); [scite record of the meta-analysis](https://scite.ai/reports/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-ynjnxJ)
- Stawarz, Cox and Blandford 2015 (CHI): a review of habit-formation apps found most rely on self-tracking and reminders; in their study reminders supported repetition but *hindered* habit formation because people became dependent on the reminder rather than a contextual cue — [Stawarz et al. 2015, UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1468224/)
- Fogg's "Tiny Habits" method (anchor, tiny behaviour, celebration) is described in secondary sources as resting on Fogg's lab work with an "over 85 percent adherence after five days" figure; I found no peer-reviewed publication of that figure — [Tiny Habits explainer (secondary)](https://goalsandprogress.com/tiny-habits-fogg-behavior-model-explained/)
- "Habit stacking" (Clear) is a popularisation of the anchoring idea; the only peer-reviewed support I found is the implementation-intention and Judah-type context-cue literature, not a direct test of the stacking recipe — [James Clear, habit stacking](https://jamesclear.com/habit-stacking)

### Inferences
- Design rule: let each person pick an existing workday event as the cue ("after I close the morning stand-up", "when I start lunch") and store that cue text; schedule the Break prompt at that routine boundary rather than a clock time. Grade: **Replicated** (context-cue and implementation-intention literature).
- Design rule: treat the first 10 weeks as the habit-formation window; do not reward or penalise single missed days (Lally's data). Grade: **Replicated**.
- Design rule: fade reminders over time (e.g., daily for 2 weeks, then only on missed days, then off) so the behaviour attaches to the context cue, not the notification. Grade: **Single-study** (Stawarz 2015, qualitative/HCI) but consistent with habit theory.
- "Habit stacking" as a label is fine for copy, but do not claim it is evidence-based beyond anchoring to an existing routine.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Gardner's reviews (Gardner, Lally and Wardle 2012 "Making health habitual"; Gardner 2015) to cite cue-type comparisons directly; the Judah study is the clearest cue-placement experiment found.
- No peer-reviewed test of Fogg's specific recipe or of the "celebration" component was found.
- Keller et al. 2021 (routine-based versus time-based cue planning) was not retrievable this session.

## Q2. Streaks: for and against; forgiveness; long-term versus short-term

### Takeaway
Logged streaks become a goal in their own right and increase persistence (Silverman and Barasch 2023, JCR; a 2025 OBHDP paper), but a broken streak reduces subsequent engagement, more so when the person blames themselves; letting people repair a streak attenuates the drop. Industry data (Duolingo) are consistent but not peer-reviewed, and the Snapchat literature shows streaks can turn into obligation and FOMO. Grade: **Mixed** overall; **Replicated** for "broken streak lowers next-day engagement unless repairable"; **Backfires** when the streak is unforgiving or socially obligating.

### Cited Findings
- Silverman and Barasch 2023, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", Journal of Consumer Research 49(6):1095-1117: logged streaks (3+ consecutive performances) make people adopt "keep the streak" as a goal; intact streaks raise the likelihood of repeating the behaviour, broken streaks lower it; the effect is amplified when the break is attributed to oneself rather than external causes and attenuated when the streak can be "repaired" — [JCR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/49/6/1095/6623414); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation); [University of Delaware profile](https://lerner.udel.edu/seeing-opportunity/lerner-professor-researches-how-streaks-motivate-us/)
- 2025 OBHDP paper "The motivating power of streaks: Increasing persistence is as easy as 1, 2, 3" (listed in Silverman's publication record) reports that as few as three consecutive performances create a streak that increases persistence — [OBHDP 187 (2025) listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v187y2025ics0749597825000032.html); [Silverman CV](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-web/owen/files/people-3750-jackiesilvermancvsep25-20250903144135.pdf)
- A 2026 Journal of Experimental Social Psychology paper reports that *collective* (group) streaks motivate prosocial behaviour — [Collective streaks motivate prosocial behavior](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022103126000715) (abstract not retrievable; details in Gaps)
- Snapchat streaks (n = 2,483 early adolescents, Belgium, mean age 13.5): problematic smartphone use was associated with streak engagement; FOMO, problematic use and lower social-media self-control correlated weakly with number and length of streaks; qualitative work reports stress and obligation from streak expectations — [Snapchat streaks study, Computers in Human Behavior Reports 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772503023000476)
- Duolingo (industry, not peer-reviewed): reports over 600 streak experiments; claims users with 7+ day streaks retain at 2.4x the rate of users without streaks, that Streak Freeze reduced churn by 21% among at-risk users, and that leniency (freezes, "weekend amulets") increased rather than decreased engagement — [Trophy.so summary of Duolingo streak work](https://trophy.so/blog/the-psychology-of-streaks-how-sylvi-weaponized-duolingos-best-feature-against-them); [Lenny's Podcast summary with Duolingo retention PM](https://www.recall.it/summary/business/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks-or-jackson-shuttleworth-group-pm-retention-team)
- Lally 2010: missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation (see Q1) — [Steps.org summary](https://steps.org/sources/lally-2010-habit-formation/)
- Patel's BE FIT and STEP UP trials used weekly point budgets with loss framing (points deducted on missed days) and levels, which function as a soft streak with a weekly reset rather than an all-or-nothing counter (see Q3) — [BE FIT, JAMA Intern Med 2017](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2655242)

### Inferences
- Streaks help short-term persistence (days to weeks) via a self-set goal; the long-term evidence is industry retention data, not RCTs. Treat them as a habit-formation scaffold for the first 2-3 months, not a permanent motivator.
- Design rules (grade: **Replicated** for the forgiveness mechanism): (1) count streaks in *active workdays*, not calendar days, so weekends and leave never break them; (2) include automatic "repair" (one free miss per week, or a 24-hour repair window), since repairability attenuates the broken-streak drop; (3) attribute misses externally in copy ("you were out Tuesday") rather than "you broke your streak"; (4) never send guilt-framed notifications about an at-risk streak.
- Consider a *team* streak ("the studio has taken a Break 14 workdays running") in addition to personal streaks; the 2026 collective-streaks paper suggests group streaks motivate prosocial effort and they diffuse individual blame.
- Prefer a weekly point budget with a reset (Patel design) over an unbounded streak counter for anything that must be sustainable for months.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the Silverman and Barasch field datasets or effect sizes (the summaries confirm direction and moderators only).
- Could not retrieve the 2025 OBHDP and 2026 JESP abstracts (sample sizes, effect sizes unknown).
- Duolingo figures are company-reported; no independent replication. No Headspace streak study was found.
- Fishbach's work on breaking streaks and the Cochran and Tesser "what the hell" effect were not retrievable this session (search budget exhausted).

## Q3. Points, badges, leaderboards (PBL); competition versus collaboration versus support

### Takeaway
PBL reliably increase *quantity* of behaviour but not intrinsic motivation (Mekler 2017), satisfy competence needs when used as feedback (Sailer 2017), and the best health-behaviour RCTs (Patel's BE FIT and STEP UP, Zhang 2016) show that small-group competition with social comparison beats support-only designs, with STEP UP's competition arm the only one whose effect persisted after the game ended. Leaderboards are **Mixed**: they act like a difficult goal for people near the top and can demotivate those at the bottom; classroom gamification with badges plus a leaderboard lowered intrinsic motivation and grades over a semester (Hanus and Fox 2015).

### Cited Findings
- Hamari, Koivisto and Sarsa 2014 ("Does gamification work?"): gamification produces positive effects, but effects depend heavily on context and users; many studies are short-term and methodologically weak — [Hamari et al. 2014, HICSS](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/does-gamification-work-a-literature-review-of-empirical-studies-o/)
- Koivisto and Hamari 2019 review of 819 studies: results lean positive but "the amount of mixed results is remarkable" — [Koivisto and Hamari 2019, Int J Information Management (open PDF)](https://www.utupub.fi/bitstream/10024/165153/1/Koivisto_Hamari_2019_International_Journal_of_Information_Management.pdf)
- Sailer, Hense, Mayr and Mandl 2017 (randomised online simulation; Computers in Human Behavior): badges, leaderboards and performance graphs increased competence need satisfaction and perceived task meaningfulness; avatars, meaningful story and teammates increased relatedness; perceived decision freedom (autonomy) could not be affected as intended — [Sailer et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074756321630855X)
- Mekler et al. 2017 (image-annotation task): points, levels and leaderboards significantly raised output quantity but did not change intrinsic motivation, perceived competence or autonomy, so they acted as extrinsic incentives — [Mekler et al. 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/281539797_Towards_understanding_the_effects_of_individual_gamification_elements_on_intrinsic_motivation_and_performance)
- Landers, Bauer and Callan (n = 339, 12-minute brainstorming task): adding a leaderboard raised performance about as much as a difficult or "impossible" assigned goal; authors warn leaderboards narrow effort and should not be a blanket fix for low performers — [Landers et al., Computers in Human Behavior](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/281325758_Gamification_of_task_performance_with_leaderboards_A_goal_setting_experiment)
- Hanus and Fox 2015 (n = 71 students, 4 time points, one semester): the gamified course (badges, leaderboard, coins) showed lower intrinsic motivation by Time 2 and lower satisfaction and empowerment over time, and the drop in intrinsic motivation mediated lower final-exam grades — [Hanus and Fox 2015, Computers & Education](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131514002000)
- Christy and Fox 2014: leaderboards in a virtual classroom affected women's maths performance via social comparison more than stereotype threat — [Christy and Fox 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131514001195)
- A 2025 study found gamifying lectures with a leaderboard reduced female students' social engagement ("more competition, less interaction") — [Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12528-025-09438-4)
- A gamified crowdsourcing study found leaderboard *position* shapes motivation through competence satisfaction (high positions) versus competence frustration (low positions) — [Internet Research 33(7)](https://www.emerald.com/intr/article/33/7/1/178330/How-leaderboard-positions-shape-our-motivation-the)
- Bai et al. 2021 ("From top to bottom") attribute inconsistent leaderboard findings to different leaderboard designs and examine how position on different leaderboard types affects performance, intrinsic motivation and engagement in online courses — [Bai et al. 2021, Computers & Education](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131521001743) (details not retrievable)
- Sailer and Homner 2020 meta-analysis of gamified learning: small-to-moderate positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioural outcomes; cognitive effects stable in high-rigour studies, motivational and behavioural effects less stable; effects depend on which elements are used — [Sailer and Homner 2020, Educ Psychol Rev](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=4225936); [gamification-research.org summary](http://gamification-research.org/2019/08/does-gamification-of-learning-work/)
- Patel et al. 2017 BE FIT (JAMA Internal Medicine; families; 12-week game, 24 weeks total): gamification arm with points, levels, a randomly selected family member whose goal determined the family's points, and loss framing; step-goal achievement 53% versus 32% in control; mean daily step increase close to one mile per person; differences persisted through 12 weeks of follow-up — [BE FIT, JAMA Intern Med 2017](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2655242); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28973115/); [Penn CHTI BE FIT page](https://chti.upenn.edu/be-fit)
- Patel et al. 2019 STEP UP (JAMA Internal Medicine; overweight/obese Deloitte employees across 40 states; 24-week intervention plus 12-week follow-up; arms: control, support (chosen sponsor got weekly updates), collaboration (teams of 3, random member represents team), competition (clusters of 3 with weekly leaderboard email)): during intervention, competition +920 steps/day versus control, support +689, collaboration +637 (all significant); at follow-up only competition remained significant (+569 steps/day) — [STEP UP, JAMA Intern Med 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2749761); [Penn Today summary](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/competition-works-better-support-or-collaboration-increase-daily-step-counts); [Penn CHTI STEP UP page](https://chti.upenn.edu/step-up)
- Patel et al. 2021 ENGAGE (economically disadvantaged adults at cardiovascular risk): tested goal-setting approaches inside the same gamification platform — [ENGAGE, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8411363/) (results not retrieved)
- Patel et al. 2016 (Annals of Internal Medicine; n = 281; 7,000-step goal; 13 weeks): proportion of goal-days was 0.30 control, 0.35 gain-framed ($1.40/day), 0.36 lottery, 0.45 loss-framed ($42 allocated monthly, $1.40 removed per missed day); all incentive effects disappeared in follow-up — [Patel et al. 2016, Annals](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/abs/10.7326/M15-1635); [CHIBE summary](https://chibe.upenn.edu/news/loss-incentive-motivates-employees-to-take-more-steps/)
- Zhang, Brackbill, Yang, Becker, Herbert and Centola 2016 (Preventive Medicine Reports; n = 790 university students; 4 arms; 11-week exercise-class attendance): attendance was 90% higher in the two social-comparison conditions (mean 1.9 classes) than in the two conditions without comparison (mean 1.0), p = 0.003; social comparison increased attendance 62% (p = 0.03) while social support alone had no significant effect (p = 0.68); the comparison effect did not depend on individual versus team incentives — [Zhang et al. 2016, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27617191/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211335516300936); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/10/161027122554.htm)
- Note: the brief's phrasing ("competitive and support social features both increased exercise 90%+") does not match the paper; support-only networks did *not* help, and the authors' earlier work suggests support networks can spread inactivity as easily as activity.

### Inferences
- Grades: Points and levels as progress feedback: **Replicated** for behaviour quantity, **Mixed/null** for intrinsic motivation. Badges: **Mixed** (competence feedback in Sailer; part of the demotivating bundle in Hanus and Fox). Individual leaderboards in a mixed-ability group: **Mixed, backfires for bottom ranks**. Small-group competition (3-6 people) with weekly reset: **Replicated** in health-behaviour RCTs (Patel 2019, Zhang 2016). Team-collaboration points where a random member represents the team: **Single-trial** (BE FIT, STEP UP), positive. Loss-framed weekly point budget: **Replicated** within Patel's programme; effects fade when the incentive is removed.
- For a 12-person studio: use clusters of 3-4 (two or three squads) with a weekly leaderboard of *squads*, not individuals; this reproduces the STEP UP competition structure (clusters of 3 with weekly ranking) while avoiding a permanent bottom-ranked person. Rotate squads monthly.
- Show each person a *personal relative* measure (percent of own weekly goal, change versus own last week) and reserve public ranking for squads; this follows Landers' warning and the competence-frustration findings.
- Keep the weekly 70-point-style budget idea: everyone starts the week with full points, loses some on missed days, and levels are earned by hitting a weekly threshold; the weekly reset is a built-in forgiveness mechanism.
- Expect behavioural effects to shrink when the game stops; the only Patel arm with carry-over was competition. Design for ongoing, low-intensity play rather than a 12-week campaign.

### Gaps
- Exact STEP UP (n) and BE FIT (n, number of families) and confidence intervals were not verifiable because JAMA and PubMed were blocked; the numbers above come from Penn press summaries.
- Sailer et al. 2017 sample size and effect sizes were not retrievable.
- Bai et al. 2021 findings on absolute versus relative leaderboards could not be read; no direct RCT of "relative progress" versus "absolute rank" leaderboards was found this session.
- Landers' Theory of Gamified Learning (2014) was located but not read in detail.

## Q4. Self-Determination Theory: controlling versus informational feedback

### Takeaway
Expected, tangible, performance-contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999: d from -0.28 to -0.40 on free-choice behaviour), whereas positive informational feedback enhances it (d = +0.33) and unexpected rewards are neutral (d = 0.01). Cerasoli 2014 adds that incentives predict quantity while intrinsic motivation predicts quality, and that the two are not necessarily antagonistic unless incentives are tightly tied to performance. Grade: **Replicated** (two large meta-analyses).

### Cited Findings
- Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999 meta-analysis (Psychological Bulletin; 128 experiments): engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28); expected tangible rewards d = -0.36; unexpected tangible rewards d = 0.01; positive verbal feedback enhanced free-choice behaviour (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31) — [Deci et al. 1999 PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); [2001 follow-up, Deci Koestner Ryan](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- Cerasoli, Nicklin and Ford 2014 (Psychological Bulletin; 183 studies, 212,468 participants): intrinsic motivation is a medium-to-strong predictor of performance; incentives directly tied to performance "crowd out" the predictive value of intrinsic motivation; intrinsic motivation predicts more unique variance in *quality*, incentives in *quantity* — [Cerasoli et al. 2014, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/); [open PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/2014_Cerasoli_Intrinsic.pdf)
- Przybylski, Rigby and Ryan 2010 (Review of General Psychology): a motivational model of game engagement in which appeal and short-term wellbeing effects depend on satisfaction of competence, autonomy and relatedness; mismatch between skill and challenge produces boredom or frustration — [Przybylski et al. 2010 PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2010_PrzybylskiRigbyRyan_ROGP.pdf)
- Sailer 2017 and Mekler 2017 (Q3) show PBL can serve competence feedback without raising intrinsic motivation; Hanus and Fox 2015 shows a reward-heavy bundle can lower it.

### Inferences
- Design rules (grade **Replicated**): make points and badges *informational* (they describe what you did and how it compares to your own past) rather than *controlling* (no pay, prizes, or manager-visible performance consequences attached). Avoid "do X to unlock Y" framing for wellbeing behaviours.
- Use occasional *unexpected* recognition (a surprise team shout-out after a good week) rather than announced contingent prizes; unexpected rewards do not undermine intrinsic motivation.
- Protect autonomy explicitly: opt-in to leaderboards and streak display; choice of which Break activity to do; no mandatory participation. Sailer could not move autonomy with game elements, so autonomy must come from product policy, not mechanics.
- Keep the Break section separate from performance review and billable metrics; Cerasoli's crowding-out result implies that tying wellbeing points to work evaluation would both reduce quality of engagement and read as surveillance.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly tests "informational versus controlling" framing of *digital* points in a workplace wellbeing app.

## Q5. Variable rewards, loot-box mechanics, dark patterns, and the ethical line

### Takeaway
Random (variable-ratio) reward schedules reliably increase engagement, but in games they are the mechanism behind loot boxes, whose spending correlates with problem gambling (Zendle and Cairns 2018, replicated 2019). Zagal's taxonomy of temporal, monetary and social-capital dark patterns, and the 2023-2026 HCI literature, treat "play by appointment", artificial scarcity, grinding, and social obligation as patterns that create frustration, coercion or regret. Grade: variable rewards for engagement **Replicated**; for wellbeing **Backfires** when they create compulsion or obligation.

### Cited Findings
- Zendle and Cairns 2018 (PLOS ONE, large survey): loot-box spending related to problem-gambling severity (η² = 0.054; subgroup differences d = 0.28 to 0.37) — [Zendle and Cairns 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0206767); replicated in 2019 — [Loot boxes are again linked to problem gambling](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6405116/)
- Zagal et al.'s framework divides dark patterns in games into temporal (e.g., "play by appointment", grinding), monetary (e.g., pay to skip) and social-capital-based (e.g., obligating friends) patterns; later work defines them as designs that create frustration, coercion or regret without informed consent, implemented to raise retention or monetisation — [A Game of Dark Patterns (CHI EA 2022)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3491101.3519837); [Level Up or Game Over (2024-25)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3701571.3701604); [Systematic review of dark patterns and random reward mechanisms in youth, 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1875952126000443)
- Dark patterns are defined as choice architectures that circumvent volitional agency and steer users toward outcomes against their long-term interests — [Level Up or Game Over, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.05039)
- Deci et al. 1999: unexpected rewards do not undermine intrinsic motivation (d = 0.01), which distinguishes occasional surprise recognition from schedule-driven variable rewards — [Deci et al. 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- Snapchat streaks produce obligation and FOMO in adolescents (Q2) — [Snapchat streaks study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772503023000476)

### Inferences
- Ethical lines for the Break section, derived from the taxonomy: no random-chance reward containers; no countdown timers or "only 2 hours left" scarcity; no notifications engineered around loss of status; no mechanics that require teammates to act so you do not lose something; no paid shortcuts; always a visible, one-click way to pause or hide streaks, points and leaderboards without penalty.
- Allowed: small *unexpected* delights (a surprise doodle or team kudos after a milestone) that are not on a schedule, are not currency, and cannot be lost.
- Grade "variable rewards" as **Backfires** for a wellbeing product even though they would raise raw engagement.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares variable versus fixed rewards specifically for wellbeing outcomes (as opposed to engagement) in a non-gambling app.
- Zagal, Björk and Lewis 2013 original paper was not fetched; the taxonomy details come from later HCI papers that cite it.

## Q6. Social features: comparison, kudos, public commitment, accountability, team versus individual goals

### Takeaway
Social comparison in small groups is the most robust social lever in field RCTs (Zhang 2016; STEP UP); low-stakes social affirmation (Strava kudos) predicts more frequent running in a longitudinal field study; support-only networks do not reliably help. Commitment devices work when voluntarily chosen. Grade: small-group comparison **Replicated**; kudos **Single-study (observational)**; support-only **Mixed/null**.

### Cited Findings
- Zhang et al. 2016 and STEP UP 2019: see Q3 — comparison beats support; competition effect persisted post-intervention in STEP UP.
- "Kudos make you run!" (Social Networks, 2022; longitudinal study of 329 runners in five virtual Strava clubs): runners who receive kudos subsequently run more often and more; kudos act as social affirmation — [Kudos make you run, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378873322000909); [Groningen repository](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/kudos-make-you-run-how-runners-influence-each-other-on-the-online/)
- Mixed-methods study of club runners: kudos and comments boosted hedonic motivation and habit for some users; others ignored or avoided the feature; "if it's not on Strava it didn't happen" pressures also reported — [Behavioral Sciences 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16020224); [Collegiate club runners study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366679956_If_It's_not_on_Strava_it_Didn't_Happen_Perceived_Psychosocial_Implications_of_Strava_use_in_Collegiate_Club_Runners)
- Rogers, Milkman and Volpp 2014 (JAMA 311:2065-66): commitment devices (deposit contracts, scheduled workouts with friends) help people lose weight, exercise, quit smoking; two defining features are that people voluntarily elect them and that they impose a cost on failure — [Rogers et al. 2014, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24777472/)
- Sailer 2017: teammates and shared narrative increased relatedness in the experiment — [Sailer et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074756321630855X)
- BE FIT: family-level collaboration (random member's goal determines family points) raised goal attainment 53% vs 32% — [BE FIT](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2655242)

### Inferences
- Build a lightweight kudos reaction on every completed Break; kudos are low cost, non-controlling and are the one social feature with field evidence of raising frequency.
- Make accountability *opt-in and voluntary* (pick a "break buddy" or sponsor), matching the commitment-device literature; do not auto-assign.
- Team goals should be *collaborative* (squad hits 80% of its Break days) with *comparison between squads*; this reproduces the combined condition in Zhang and the collaboration/competition arms in Patel.
- Avoid publishing individual non-participation; the STEP UP support arm used a private sponsor, not public shaming.

### Gaps
- No RCT on "likes/kudos" as a manipulated variable was found; the Strava evidence is observational.
- Public-commitment experiments (e.g., Hollenbeck) were not retrievable in this session.

## Q7. Fresh-start effect, temptation bundling, implementation intentions, tiny goals, identity framing

### Takeaway
Fresh starts (Dai, Milkman and Riis 2014) and temptation bundling (Milkman 2014; Kirgios 2020 at scale) have replicated field evidence; implementation intentions have a d = 0.65 meta-analysis; identity framing ("be a voter") failed large pre-registered replications and should be graded **Mixed/contested**.

### Cited Findings
- Dai, Milkman and Riis 2014 (Management Science 60(10):2563-2582): Google searches for "diet", gym visits and goal commitments all rise after temporal landmarks (new week, month, year, semester, birthdays, holidays); proposed mechanism is new mental accounting periods that relegate past failures to the previous period — [Dai et al. 2014, Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901); [open PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Dai-Milkman-Riis_FreshStartEffect_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)
- Milkman, Minson and Volpp 2014 (Management Science 60(2):283-299; n = 226): participants whose tempting audiobooks were locked to the gym attended 51% more often than controls during the 10-week study; the advantage faded after the Thanksgiving break — [Milkman et al. 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v60y2014i2p283-299.html)
- Kirgios, Mandel, Milkman et al. 2020 (OBHDP 161:20-35; n = 6,792): participants given an audiobook plus encouragement to temptation-bundle were 10-14% more likely to work out weekly than controls, persisting to 17 weeks post-intervention — [Kirgios et al. 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346246007_Teaching_temptation_bundling_to_boost_exercise_A_field_experiment)
- Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006: d = 0.65 (Q1).
- Bryan et al. 2011 ("be a voter" noun framing) versus Gerber, Huber, Biggers and Hendry 2016: the replication used more than 2,000 participants per condition (versus about 200 total in the larger original study) and found no turnout effect; Bryan et al. 2016 (PNAS) argued the replications were "psychologically inauthentic"; independent commentary argues the original was likely p-hacked — [Gerber et al. 2016 PDF](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/85000/1/07_GerberEtAl2016-SubtleLinguisticCuesMayNotAffectVoterBehavior_Final.pdf); [Bryan et al. 2016, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1609625113); [Replicability Index critique](https://replicationindex.com/2019/12/02/christopher-j-bryan-claims-replicators-p-hack-to-get-non-significant-results-i-claim-he-p-hacked-his-original-results/); [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/9347_Voter-behavior-unaffected-by-subtle-linguistic-changes_United-States_Dec2020.pdf)
- Patel 2016: loss-framed daily incentive outperformed gain and lottery framing but all effects vanished after incentives stopped (Q3).

### Inferences
- Design rules: (1) use Mondays, the first of the month, and post-holiday returns as "fresh start" moments to invite people to (re)set a Break goal; message a reset on those days rather than nagging mid-week (**Replicated**). (2) Bundle the Break with something people already want (a curated playlist, a 5-minute casual game, the daily team puzzle) so the Break is the only place that treat appears (**Replicated**). (3) Ask each person to write an if-then plan in their own words at onboarding and show it back on their cue (**Replicated**). (4) Do not rely on identity labels ("you're a breaker") as a motivational mechanism; harmless as copy, unsupported as a lever (**Mixed**).
- Keep goals tiny and self-chosen ("one 3-minute Break on 4 of 5 workdays"); goal-setting theory and Patel's step-goal designs both start from a personal baseline plus a modest increase.

### Gaps
- The brief mentions "Hafner et al." on reminders and timing; I could not identify or retrieve that paper.
- No study on implementation intentions implemented *inside* an app in a workplace setting was located.

## Q8. Digital wellbeing app evidence and attrition curves

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses find small effects of mental-health and mindfulness apps on depression, anxiety and stress (g roughly 0.28 to 0.46), but real-world retention is brutal: median 15-day retention of 3.9% and 30-day retention of 3.3% across popular mental-health apps (Baumel 2019), with more than an 80% drop between day 1 and day 10. Trial-level feature moderators (in-app feedback, mood monitoring) did not explain effect sizes in Linardon 2019. Grade: efficacy **Replicated (small)**; attrition **Replicated**.

### Cited Findings
- Linardon, Cuijpers, Carlbring, Messer and Fuller-Tyszkiewicz 2019 (World Psychiatry; RCTs): apps outperformed controls for depression (g = 0.28, 41 trials) and generalised anxiety (g = 0.30, 28 trials); no evidence that in-app feedback, mood monitoring or theoretical orientation moderated effects; larger effects when no in-person feedback was provided — [Linardon et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wps.20673); 2024 update of 176 RCTs — [Linardon et al. 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wps.21183)
- Perceived stress meta-analysis (22 RCTs): g = 0.35 (95% CI 0.21-0.48) favouring apps — [Efficacy of mental health apps on stress, Health Psychology Review 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2024.2379784)
- Gál, Ștefan and Cristea 2021 (Journal of Affective Disorders; 34 RCTs, 7,612 participants): mindfulness apps g = 0.33 depression, g = 0.28 anxiety (15 trials each), g = 0.46 perceived stress at post-test and g = 0.35 at follow-up; participants completed on average 43% of recommended sessions — [Gál et al. 2021](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344672612_The_efficacy_of_mindfulness_meditation_apps_in_enhancing_users'_well-being_and_mental_health_related_outcomes_a_meta-analysis_of_randomized_controlled_trials)
- Baumel, Muench, Edan and Kane 2019 (JMIR; panel usage data for popular Google Play mental-health apps): median day-1 open rate 69.4%, day-15 retention 3.9% (IQR 10.3%), day-30 retention 3.3% (IQR 6.2%); >80% decline between day 1 and day 10; peer-support (8.9%), tracker (6.1%) and mindfulness (4.7%) apps retained better at day 30 than breathing apps (0.0%) — [Baumel et al. 2019, JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/e14567/PDF)
- Headspace daily-diary study in students: more time on Headspace predicted day-to-day improvements in mindfulness, depression, anxiety and stress — [Mind the app, 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/28324765.2024.2400878)

### Inferences
- A 12-person team product does not face anonymous consumer churn, but the same curve applies to any optional feature; plan explicit re-engagement at day 3, day 10 and the first Monday after a lapse, and measure 30-day active use as the honest KPI.
- Peer-support and tracker formats retain best in Baumel's data; a shared team feed with reactions (peer support) plus a simple personal log (tracker) is the retention-friendly skeleton, with breathing-only content as an option rather than the core.
- Expect small wellbeing effects; the honest value proposition is "a reliable short recovery break and a shared ritual", not clinical change.

### Gaps
- No RCT isolates which app features (reminders, personalisation, social) causally improve retention; Linardon found no feature moderators for *efficacy*. Retention moderators are mostly observational.

## Q9. Gamification for wellbeing: Johnson 2016, Cheng 2019, Litvin 2020

### Takeaway
Reviews find gamified health and wellbeing interventions mostly report positive or mixed effects but with low-quality evidence (Johnson 2016: 59% positive, 41% mixed); mental-health app gamification is common (levels, points, rewards, narrative, personalisation) but rarely evaluated as a manipulated variable (Cheng 2019). The one RCT found (Litvin 2020, n = 358) showed a gamified app raised resilience and wellbeing and retained 21% more participants than a CBT journal app. Grade: **Mixed**, with one supportive single RCT.

### Cited Findings
- Johnson, Deterding, Kuhn, Staneva, Stoyanov and Hides 2016 (Internet Interventions 6:89-106): 19 empirical papers; 59% positive, 41% mixed effects; mostly moderate or lower quality; clearer effects for health behaviours than for cognitive outcomes — [Johnson et al. 2016, York](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/gamification-for-health-and-wellbeing-a-systematic-review-of-the-/); [Mental Elf summary](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/commissioning/technology/gamification-for-health-and-wellbeing/)
- Cheng, Davenport, Johnson, Vella and Hickie 2019 (JMIR Mental Health 6(6):e13717): most common elements in mental-health apps are levels/progress, points, rewards, narrative/theme, personalisation and customisation; gamification is rarely isolated and evaluated — [Cheng et al. 2019, JMIR Mental Health](https://mental.jmir.org/2019/6/e13717/)
- Litvin, Saunders, Maier and Lüttke 2020 (PLOS ONE; n = 358; 5-week 3-arm RCT: gamified eQuoo app vs CBT journal app vs waitlist): significant increases in resilience and wellbeing and decreased anxiety versus both controls; with 90% adherence the gamified app retained 21% more participants than controls — [Litvin et al. 2020, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0237220); [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10109330/)
- A 2023 scoping review of gamified digital interventions for mental-health prevention in adults summarises the field as promising but heterogeneous — [BMC Public Health 2023 scoping review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-023-17517-3)

### Inferences
- Gamification is most defensible as an *adherence* tool (Litvin's 21% retention gain; BE FIT/STEP UP), not as the source of wellbeing benefit; pair it with content that has its own evidence (short casual games, breathing, movement, a social puzzle).
- The elements Cheng found most common (progress, points, narrative, personalisation) are also the ones Sailer linked to competence and relatedness; narrative/theme is cheap and low-risk for a small team product.

### Gaps
- No RCT isolates individual gamification elements in a *workplace* wellbeing tool.

## Q10. The daily shared puzzle pattern (Wordle)

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study of Wordle itself was found; the evidence-based reading of why it worked combines mechanisms documented elsewhere in these notes: one puzzle per day (bounded play, no variable-reward loop), identical content for everyone (enables low-stakes social comparison), spoiler-free shareable results (social affirmation and relatedness), and a difficulty sweet spot (competence). Grade: **Inference**, supported by replicated mechanisms but not by direct studies.

### Cited Findings
- Psychologists' commentary: one game per day removes the "never-ending" reward loop of Candy Crush-type games; everyone solves the same word, so results are directly comparable; the emoji grid shares outcome without spoiling; most people solve in 4-5 guesses, the "sweet spot between too easy and too hard" — [Psychology Today, Why is Wordle so popular?](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/psychology-tomorrow/202201/why-is-wordle-so-popular); [UX Magazine on Wordle](https://uxmag.com/articles/the-fascinating-psychology-tricks-that-make-wordle-so-addictive); [Inc. summary of psychologists' explanations](https://www.inc.com/jessica-stillman/wordle-psychology-pandemic-connection.html)
- Mechanism support from elsewhere in these notes: social comparison in small groups raises behaviour (Zhang 2016; STEP UP); competence feedback via performance graphs (Sailer 2017); kudos as affirmation (Strava); bounded daily play avoids temporal dark patterns ("play by appointment" is listed as a dark pattern when it *restricts* play to coerce return, which is different from a voluntary once-a-day ritual) — see Q3, Q5, Q6.

### Inferences
- For a 12-person team: ship one shared daily puzzle (same for everyone), 1-3 minutes, with a spoiler-free result card (guess count or time bucket, not the answer) posted to the team feed with kudos reactions. Do not add a second puzzle on demand; the scarcity is the point and it is not coercive because nothing is lost by skipping.
- Show the team distribution (how many solved in 2, 3, 4 guesses) rather than a ranked list; this gives comparison without a bottom rank.
- Treat the daily puzzle as a *relatedness ritual*, not cognitive training (see Part 2).

### Gaps
- No academic study of Wordle's engagement or wellbeing effects was found; the search budget ran out before a targeted scholarly search could be run.

## Q11. Working memory and "brain training": what the meta-analyses and the FTC say

### Takeaway
Commercial brain training and n-back/Cogmed-style working-memory training improve the trained tasks and near-transfer tasks but show no reliable far transfer to intelligence, academic skills or everyday cognition once active controls and publication bias are accounted for (Melby-Lervåg, Redick and Hulme 2016; Simons et al. 2016; Sala and Gobet 2019: g = 0.001). Lumosity paid $2 million to settle FTC deceptive-advertising charges in 2016. The ACTIVE trial is the main exception claimed (speed-of-processing training and dementia incidence), and it is contested. Grade: far transfer **Null/replicated null**.

### Cited Findings
- Melby-Lervåg, Redick and Hulme 2016 (Perspectives on Psychological Science; 87 publications, 145 comparisons): reliable short-term gains on intermediate transfer (verbal and visuospatial WM) but no convincing far transfer to nonverbal ability, verbal ability, decoding, reading comprehension or arithmetic versus treated controls; degree of WM improvement did not predict far-transfer gains; passive controls inflate effects — [Melby-Lervåg et al. 2016, Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:daa9a729-f17b-4e45-97c0-cff5075ea404); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033)
- Simons, Boot, Charness, Gathercole, Chabris, Hambrick and Stine-Morrow 2016 (Psychological Science in the Public Interest 17(3):103-186): extensive evidence that brain-training improves performance on trained tasks, less that it improves closely related tasks, and little that it improves distantly related tasks or everyday cognition; no cited study met all best-practice criteria — [Simons et al. 2016, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27697851/); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/10/161003113427.htm)
- Sala, Aksayli, Tatlidil, Tatsumi, Gondo and Gobet 2019 (Collabra; second-order meta-analysis): after controlling placebo effects and publication bias, far-transfer effect and true variance equal zero across populations and programmes; with active controls g = 0.001 (SE 0.055, p = .98) — [Sala et al. 2019](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/near-and-far-transfer-in-cognitive-training-a-second-order-meta-a/); Gobet and Sala 2023 "Cognitive training: a field in search of a phenomenon" — [Perspectives on Psychological Science 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17456916221091830)
- WM training in older adults: comprehensive meta-analysis finds no enhancement of cognitive skills — [Intelligence 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160289619301680); in typically developing children, multilevel meta-analysis likewise finds no far transfer — [Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-019-01681-y)
- FTC v. Lumos Labs (January 2016): Lumosity claimed 10-15 minutes, 3-4 times a week would improve work and school performance and delay age-related decline; the company paid $2 million, had to notify subscribers and offer easy cancellation, and must hold "competent and reliable scientific evidence" for future claims — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)
- ACTIVE trial (n = 2,802 healthy older adults, 6 sites; memory, reasoning, speed-of-processing arms vs no-contact control): Rebok et al. 2014 reported reasoning and speed (not memory) training maintained targeted cognitive gains at 10 years; all three groups self-reported less IADL difficulty (speed training effect size 0.36) but no differences on performance-based everyday activities — [Rebok et al. 2014, J Am Geriatr Soc](https://www.academia.edu/13510963/Ten_Year_Effects_of_the_Advanced_Cognitive_Training_for_Independent_and_Vital_Elderly_Cognitive_Training_Trial_on_Cognition_and_Everyday_Functioning_in_Older_Adults)
- Edwards et al. 2017 (Alzheimer's & Dementia TRCI): speed-of-processing training associated with 29% lower dementia risk at 10 years vs control; memory and reasoning training did not reduce risk; each additional speed session associated with roughly 10% lower hazard; dementia was defined from a composite of cognitive and functional indicators and self/proxy reports, not clinical diagnosis — [Edwards et al. 2017](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.trci.2017.09.002); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29201994/); [USF press release](https://hscweb3.hsc.usf.edu/blog/2017/11/13/intervention-becomes-first-to-successfully-reduce-risk-of-dementia/)
- A 2026 ACTIVE follow-up uses claims-based dementia diagnoses over 20 years — [Coe et al. 2026, Alzheimer's & Dementia TRCI](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/trc2.70197) (results not retrieved)
- Speed-of-processing training in MCI: a 2-year RCT reported daily-function outcomes versus a control training protocol — [Wadley et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/13872877251351341) (results not retrieved)

### Inferences
- Do not market any Break game as "brain training" or claim it improves memory, focus or productivity; the FTC order and the meta-analyses make that claim both unsupported and legally risky.
- If an n-back or memory game is included, present it as a game, with gains described only as "your score on this game".
- The ACTIVE speed-training result is a single large trial with a non-clinical dementia outcome, no active control, and critics (Simons et al. 2016) who note the lack of best-practice safeguards; for a working-age creative team it is irrelevant to product claims.

### Gaps
- Edwards 2017 hazard ratios and confidence intervals could not be verified (PubMed blocked); only the 29% figure appeared in summaries.
- Boot and Simons' placebo/expectation critique (Boot et al. 2013; Foroughi et al. 2016) was not retrievable this session.
- The ACTIVE trial sample is reported as 2,802 in press materials; Rebok 2014 uses the enrolled total (not verified here).

## Q12. What does transfer: exercise, sleep, action video games, music, bilingualism, nature

### Takeaway
Aerobic and resistance exercise is the only intervention with replicated, meta-analytic evidence of broad cognitive benefit (Northey 2018: g = 0.29 in adults over 50; Erickson 2011: about 2% anterior hippocampal volume gain in one year). Action video games show medium effects in habitual players (g = 0.55) and smaller training effects (g = 0.34; attention g = 0.31; spatial g = 0.45), but Hilgard, Sala, Boot and Simons 2019 show that after proper publication-bias correction the training evidence is minimal. Music training and bilingualism do not show far transfer once design quality is controlled. Grade: exercise **Replicated**; action games **Mixed/contested**; music and bilingualism **Null when controlled**.

### Cited Findings
- Northey, Cherbuin, Pumpa, Smee and Rattray 2018 (British Journal of Sports Medicine; 36 studies, 333 effect sizes, adults >50): exercise improved cognition, effect size 0.29 (95% CI 0.17-0.41); aerobic, resistance, multicomponent and tai chi all significant; 45-60 minute sessions at moderate or higher intensity associated with benefit; results independent of cognitive domain and baseline status — [Northey et al. 2018, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28438770/)
- Erickson et al. 2011 (PNAS; RCT, n = 120 older adults, 1 year): aerobic walking increased left and right hippocampal volume by 2.12% and 1.97% while the stretching control lost 1.40% and 1.43%; volume gains related to BDNF and spatial-memory improvement — [ScienceDaily summary of Erickson 2011](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110131153249.htm); a PNAS letter disputed whether the memory improvement was attributable to exercise or hippocampal volume — [PNAS letter](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1102593108)
- Bediou, Adams, Mayer, Tipton, Green and Bavelier 2018 (Psychological Bulletin): habitual action gamers g = 0.55 over non-gamers; intervention studies g = 0.34; top-down attention g = 0.31 and spatial cognition g = 0.45 after training versus control games; publication-bias correction suggested about 30% overestimation — [Bediou et al. 2018](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Meta-Analysis-of-Action-Video-Game-Impact-on-and-Bediou-Adams/db92415ee7a5b787ae00c07894187d56aaa6cd2f)
- Hilgard, Sala, Boot and Simons 2019 (Collabra): re-analysis of Bediou 2018 with more appropriate bias adjustments found minimal evidence of transfer; identified "salami slicing" (overlapping samples across papers) — [Hilgard et al. 2019](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/5/1/30/113031/Overestimation-of-Action-Game-Training-Effects)
- A 2023 updated meta-analysis of action-game training on cognitive skills exists (Technology, Mind, and Behavior) — [Effects of Action Video Game Play on Cognitive Skills, 2023](https://tmb.apaopen.org/pub/qj0c4ij2/download/pdf) (numbers not retrieved)
- Sala and Gobet 2020 music meta-analysis: no transfer (g = 0.06), null when random allocation and active controls are accounted for — [Sala and Gobet 2020, Memory & Cognition](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-020-01060-2); Bigand and Tillmann reanalysis finds a small effect g = 0.23 with different inclusion rules — [Near and far transfer: is music special?](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-021-01226-6)
- Lehtonen et al. 2018 and Donnelly et al. 2019 meta-analyses: bilingualism's effect on executive function not significantly different from zero; Paap et al. 2025 argue bilingualism, like other brain training, does not produce far transfer — [Paap et al. 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/13670069231214599); [updated quantitative analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343558787_The_effects_of_bilingualism_on_executive_functions_An_updated_quantitative_analysis)
- Sleep: Lim and Dinges 2010 meta-analysis (70 articles, 147 tests) shows short-term sleep deprivation has its largest effect on lapses of simple attention (g = 0.78) and psychomotor speed, and essentially none on reasoning accuracy (g = 0.13, n.s.) — [Lim and Dinges 2010 PDF](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/LimDinges2010MetaAnalysis.pdf)

### Inferences
- The highest-value "cognitive" Break content is a movement break (walk, stairs, mobility), not a puzzle. If the product nudges one thing for cognition, nudge movement; everything else is for mood and relatedness.
- Action-game-style attention training is not worth building for cognitive transfer; a fast casual game is justified by mood/recovery evidence (Q14), not by attention gains.
- Sleep affects attention far more than any game can; a daily reaction benchmark (Q15) that reflects sleep is more useful self-knowledge than a trained score.

### Gaps
- No nature-exposure meta-analysis was retrieved (search budget exhausted); the brief's "nature" item is unaddressed.
- The 2023 updated action-game meta-analysis numbers were not read.

## Q13. Puzzles and crosswords: do they help cognition?

### Takeaway
Crossword use is associated with later onset of accelerated memory decline in people who go on to develop dementia (Pillai 2011: 2.54-year delay, observational), and one RCT in mild cognitive impairment found home crosswords beat a commercial cognitive-games platform on ADAS-Cog at 12 and 78 weeks with less brain atrophy (Devanand 2022, n = 107). Causal evidence in healthy working-age adults is absent. Grade: **Single-trial** in MCI; **Observational** for dementia delay; **No evidence** for healthy adults.

### Cited Findings
- Pillai et al. 2011 (JINS; Bronx Aging Study; 488 initially intact older adults, 101 developed dementia): any crossword participation delayed onset of accelerated memory decline by 2.54 years, interpreted via cognitive reserve — [Pillai et al. 2011, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22040899/)
- Devanand et al. 2022 (NEJM Evidence; COGIT; n = 107 MCI, mean age 71, two sites; 12 weeks training then boosters to 78 weeks; crosswords vs computerised cognitive games, both web-delivered): crosswords superior on ADAS-Cog at 12 and 78 weeks, superior on functional (FAQ) at 78 weeks, and less hippocampal/cortical shrinkage on MRI at 78 weeks — [Devanand et al. 2022, NEJM Evidence](https://evidence.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/EVIDe2200276); [NYP summary](https://www.nyp.org/advances/article/psychiatry/crossword-puzzles-beat-computerized-games-in-slowing-memory-loss); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/crossword-aging-memory-21741/)
- A larger follow-up trial (COGIT-2, 78 weeks, multi-site) is registered — [COGIT-2 protocol](https://www.ijclinicaltrials.com/index.php/ijct/article/view/873); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06601933](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06601933)

### Inferences
- For a team of working-age adults, crosswords and word puzzles are justified as enjoyable, low-stakes, shareable breaks, not as cognitive protection; copy should not imply dementia prevention.
- Word puzzles are the one puzzle format with any RCT evidence against a commercial brain-game platform, so if the team wants "something with evidence", a daily crossword-style or word-association puzzle is a defensible choice over abstract speed games.

### Gaps
- No RCT of puzzles in healthy adults under 60 was found.

## Q14. Casual games and mood; Tetris; gaming breaks and recovery; micro-breaks

### Takeaway
Short casual-game play reliably improves mood and reduces stress in lab RCTs (Russoniello 2009; Rupp 2017: a 5-minute Sushi Cat break beat guided relaxation on affective restoration and beat silent rest, which increased worry); prescribed 30-minute sessions reduced depression and anxiety symptoms in small RCTs (Russoniello/Fish 2013-2014). Micro-breaks meta-analysis: small but reliable gains in vigor (d = 0.36) and fatigue (d = 0.35), with performance benefits only for low-demand tasks and longer breaks. Tetris after trauma reduces intrusive memories in the short term; a multi-lab replication found the immediate effect but not reduced daily intrusions. Grade: casual games for mood **Replicated (small samples)**; micro-breaks **Replicated**; Tetris intrusion effect **Mixed**.

### Cited Findings
- Russoniello, O'Brien and Parks 2009 (RCT; Bejeweled 2 for about 25 minutes; EEG and HRV): EEG changes consistent with improved mood; HRV changes consistent with autonomic relaxation; three casual games (Bejeweled 2, Peggle, Bookworm Adventures) each showed distinct alpha-wave patterns — [Russoniello et al. 2009, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19592761/); [Effectiveness of casual video games in improving mood](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/289131468_The_effectiveness_of_casual_video_games_in_improving_mood_and_decreasing_stress)
- Russoniello, Fish and O'Brien 2013 (Games for Health; n = 59; PHQ-9 screened): casual games 3 x 30 minutes per week for one month vs browsing the NIMH depression page; reduced depressive symptoms — [Russoniello et al. 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1089/g4h.2013.0010); Fish, Russoniello and O'Brien 2014 found the same prescription reduced anxiety symptoms — [Fish et al. 2014](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275281920_The_Efficacy_of_Prescribed_Casual_Videogame_Play_in_Reducing_Symptoms_of_Anxiety_A_Randomized_Controlled_Study)
- Pine, Fleming, McCallum and Sutcliffe 2020 systematic review of casual games for anxiety, depression, stress and low mood — [Pine et al. 2020, Games for Health](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1089/g4h.2019.0132)
- Pine et al. 2020 adolescent study: contrary to prior literature, Tetris did *not* reduce physiological or psychological stress; authors suspect difficulty exceeded skill and produced anxiety; 91% of 13-15-year-olds play casual games, most commonly to feel relaxed (68%) or calm (60%) — [Pine et al. 2020, Digital Health](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2055207620949391)
- Rupp, Sweetman, Sosa, Smither and McConnell 2017 (Human Factors; n = 66; cognitive fatigue induced, then 5-minute break): casual game (Sushi Cat) produced greater engagement and affective restoration than guided relaxation; guided relaxation reduced negative affect and distress; silent rest left people less engaged and more worried — [ScienceDaily summary of Rupp 2017](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/07/170725100704.htm); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Searching-for-Affective-and-Cognitive-Restoration:-Rupp-Sweetman/fb948e4ca2d7b9748723257a07dde25637d8837b)
- Reinecke 2009: survey evidence that games are used to recover from stress and strain, including at work ("Games at work", CyberPsychology & Behavior 12(4):461-465; "Games and recovery", Journal of Media Psychology 21(3):126-142) — [Reinecke 2009, Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Games-at-Work:-The-Recreational-Use-of-Computer-Reinecke/d72180165c1b7f4dd150847e0b2b41841019fda7)
- Collins and Cox 2014 (International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 72:654-662): digital game play after work associated with post-work recovery — [Collins and Cox 2014, UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1418103/1/1-s2.0-S1071581913002012-main.pdf); a JMIR Mental Health study compared digital games with mindfulness apps for post-work recovery — [JMIR Mental Health, games vs mindfulness](https://doi.org/10.2196/12853); CHI 2023 on immersion and post-work recovery — [Gaming for Post-Work Recovery](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3544548.3581510)
- Albulescu et al. 2022 (PLOS ONE; 22 studies): micro-breaks increased vigor (d = 0.36) and reduced fatigue (d = 0.35); no significant overall performance effect; performance gains only for less cognitively demanding tasks; longer breaks gave larger performance boosts; recovering from highly depleting tasks may need more than 10 minutes — [Albulescu et al. 2022, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0272460)
- Iyadurai et al. 2018 (Molecular Psychiatry 23:674-682; ED patients after motor-vehicle accidents): Tetris after a reminder cue vs activity log reduced intrusive memories over the following week — [Iyadurai et al. 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/mp201723); 2021 mixed-trauma pilot in the ED — [Kanstrup et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-020-01124-6); a multi-lab replication with a trauma film found Tetris reduced *immediate* but not subsequent daily intrusions — [Collabra multi-lab replication](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/11/1/130791/207825/Evidence-That-Tetris-Reduces-Immediate-but-Not)

### Inferences
- Design rule (**Replicated**, small samples): offer a 3-5 minute casual game with low difficulty and no fail state as a *default* Break option; the Rupp result argues it restores affect better than guided relaxation and much better than doing nothing, and the Pine adolescent null warns that difficulty must stay below skill.
- Offer guided breathing/relaxation as an alternative for people who want lower arousal; both beat silent rest.
- Set expectations: micro-breaks improve vigor and fatigue, not output; do not sell them as productivity boosters, and encourage longer breaks (10+ minutes) after heavy creative sessions.
- Tetris-type games are not a trauma intervention in a work tool; cite the mood/recovery literature instead.

### Gaps
- Rupp 2017 effect sizes were not retrievable.
- The brief's "Pine et al. 2020 Tetris and anxiety" appears to refer to the adolescent study above (which found no stress reduction), not an RCT showing benefit; no Pine Tetris-anxiety RCT was found.

## Q15. Attention and reaction benchmarks: is a daily 1-3 minute test useful self-knowledge?

### Takeaway
The Psychomotor Vigilance Test is the validated fatigue measure; the 3-minute PVT-B (Basner and Dinges 2011) tracked total and partial sleep deprivation as well as the 10-minute PVT once the lapse threshold was lowered to 355 ms, and smartphone/tablet versions track sleep loss. Sleep loss hits simple-attention lapses hardest (g = 0.78), so a daily 1-3 minute reaction benchmark is a meaningful sleep/fatigue mirror; some later work questions the 3-minute version's convergent validity. Grade: PVT-B **Replicated** (with a caveat).

### Cited Findings
- Basner, Mollicone and Dinges 2011 (Acta Astronautica; 74 healthy adults; 33 hours total sleep deprivation, n = 31, and 5 nights of 4 hours in bed, n = 43): with the lapse threshold reduced from 500 to 355 ms, PVT-B showed no differential sensitivity to sleep loss versus the 10-minute PVT; authors call for further validation in applied settings — [Basner et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/Basner2011-ValidityandsensitivityofabriefPVT.pdf); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094576511002256)
- 3-minute smartphone and tablet PVTs showed parallel impairment profiles across 38 hours of sleep deprivation and recovery (lapses, mean RT, false starts) — [Behavior Research Methods 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-016-0763-8)
- A later study found the 3-minute PVT had inadequate convergent validity relative to the 10-minute PVT across sleep loss and recovery — [PMC8885985](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8885985/); an adaptive-duration PVT-BA accurately tracked sleep restriction — [Sleep Advances 2022](https://academic.oup.com/sleepadvances/article/3/1/zpac038/6764767)
- Lim and Dinges 2010: lapses in simple attention g = 0.78 under short-term sleep deprivation; reasoning accuracy unaffected — [Lim and Dinges 2010](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/LimDinges2010MetaAnalysis.pdf)
- Free implementations exist (PsyToolkit PVT-B; Millisecond) — [PsyToolkit PVT-B](https://www.psytoolkit.org/experiment-library/pvtb.html); [Millisecond PVT](https://www.millisecond.com/library/psychomotorvigilancetest)

### Inferences
- A 1-3 minute PVT-style "reaction check" is the one benchmark worth shipping: present it as "how rested are you today", chart it against the person's own baseline, and *never* rank it across people (it is a fatigue measure, not an ability measure).
- Use lapses (>355 ms) and mean 1/RT as the displayed metrics, per PVT-B; keep stimulus timing random (2-10 s) to avoid anticipation.
- Stroop and digit span are less sensitive to sleep loss than simple attention (Lim and Dinges), so they add novelty but not better self-knowledge; include them only as games.

### Gaps
- No study of daily consumer self-administered PVT in office workers was found; validation is lab and field (aviation, spaceflight) based.

## Q16. Creativity training and "games that teach creativity"

### Takeaway
Structured creativity training works (Scott, Leritz and Mumford 2004: 70 studies, overall effect about 0.68, strongest for programmes teaching cognitive techniques and heuristics with realistic domain exercises). Practice on the Remote Associates Test improves RAT scores; evidence that RAT or divergent-thinking *games* transfer to real creative work is thin. Grade: creativity training via cognitive techniques **Replicated**; creativity *games* **Single-study/insufficient**.

### Cited Findings
- Scott, Leritz and Mumford 2004 (Creativity Research Journal 16(4):361-388; 70 studies): well-designed training produces gains generalising across criteria, settings and populations; overall effect about 0.68 ("moderately strong"); more successful programmes focused on cognitive skills and heuristics with realistic, domain-appropriate exercises — [Scott et al. 2004, Nebraska](https://experts.nebraska.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-creativity-training-a-quantitative-review); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-effectiveness-of-creativity-training:-A-review-Scott-Leritz/dceda3e2e0cdd19a53be234566abe0f34caca2c2)
- Systematic review of RAT studies 2000-2019: RAT is widely used as a measure of remote association and insight; studies report that intensive RAT training (e.g., 10 days) raises RAT scores; the review frames RAT as a measurement tool, not a validated training intervention — [Systematic review of RAT studies, PMC7644781](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7644781/)
- Landers et al.: a leaderboard on a 12-minute "uses for a knife" divergent-thinking task raised idea output (quantity) — [Landers et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/281325758_Gamification_of_task_performance_with_leaderboards_A_goal_setting_experiment)

### Inferences
- A daily creativity prompt (alternate uses, remote associates, "worst idea" or constraint prompts) is defensible as practice of *techniques* the meta-analysis supports, especially if prompts are tied to real agency work (briefs, client categories). Do not claim it raises creativity in general.
- Make creativity prompts collaborative (team posts answers to the same prompt) to exploit the relatedness and comparison effects rather than the (unproven) transfer effect.

### Gaps
- No RCT of a creativity "game" app with far-transfer outcomes was found; the search budget ended before a targeted search.

## Q17. Mechanic grading summary and concrete design rules for a 12-person studio

### Takeaway
Keep what has replicated field evidence (small-group comparison, collaborative team points, weekly point budgets with forgiveness, informational feedback, kudos, fresh-start resets, if-then cues, bounded daily shared puzzle, casual-game and movement breaks, a personal PVT-style rested-ness check) and drop what backfires (individual leaderboards in a mixed-ability team, unforgiving streaks, contingent prizes, variable-ratio rewards, scarcity timers, guilt notifications, brain-training claims).

### Cited Findings
- Grades consolidate the sources cited in Q1-Q16; key anchors: Zhang 2016 and STEP UP (comparison > support) — [Zhang 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27617191/); [STEP UP](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/competition-works-better-support-or-collaboration-increase-daily-step-counts); Deci 1999 (controlling rewards undermine, feedback enhances) — [Deci et al. 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); Hanus and Fox 2015 (reward-heavy bundle backfires) — [Hanus and Fox 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131514002000); Silverman and Barasch 2023 (repairable streaks) — [JCR](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/49/6/1095/6623414); Rupp 2017 (5-minute casual game restores affect) — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/07/170725100704.htm); Sala and Gobet 2019 (no far transfer) — [Collabra](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/near-and-far-transfer-in-cognitive-training-a-second-order-meta-a/); Basner 2011 (PVT-B) — [PDF](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/Basner2011-ValidityandsensitivityofabriefPVT.pdf)

### Inferences
Mechanic grades:
- Workday streak with automatic repair and external attribution: **Mixed, use with forgiveness** (Silverman and Barasch; Lally; Duolingo industry data).
- Unforgiving calendar-day streak with loss notifications: **Backfires** (broken-streak drop; Snapchat obligation).
- Team (collective) streak: **Single-study**, promising.
- Points as progress feedback relative to own baseline: **Replicated** for behaviour quantity.
- Points tied to prizes, pay or performance review: **Backfires** (Deci 1999; Cerasoli 2014).
- Badges for milestones (informational): **Mixed**; harmless if not the core loop.
- Individual leaderboard across the whole studio: **Backfires for bottom ranks** (Landers; competence-frustration; Hanus and Fox).
- Squad leaderboard (clusters of 3-4, weekly reset): **Replicated** (STEP UP; Zhang).
- Collaborative squad points with random-member rule: **Single-trial, positive** (BE FIT; STEP UP collaboration arm).
- Weekly loss-framed point budget: **Replicated** within one research group; fades when removed.
- Kudos reactions: **Single-study, observational** (Strava).
- Opt-in accountability buddy: **Replicated** as commitment device.
- Fresh-start messaging on Mondays/month starts/after leave: **Replicated**.
- If-then cue chosen by the user: **Replicated**.
- Temptation bundling (treat available only during Break): **Replicated**.
- Variable/random rewards, scarcity timers, FOMO notifications: **Backfires** for wellbeing.
- Identity labels as a lever: **Mixed/contested**.
- One shared daily puzzle with spoiler-free share card: **Inference from replicated mechanisms**.
- 3-5 minute low-difficulty casual game break: **Replicated (small samples)**.
- Guided breathing/relaxation break: **Replicated** (apps meta-analyses; Rupp).
- Movement break nudges: **Replicated** (Northey; Erickson).
- Brain-training games with cognitive claims: **Null; legal risk** (Sala and Gobet; FTC).
- Daily PVT-B rested-ness check, self-referenced only: **Replicated** instrument.
- Creativity technique prompts: **Replicated** for technique training; **unproven** as games.

Design rules for the Break section:
1. Cue, not clock: each person picks a routine boundary as the Break cue; reminders fade after two weeks.
2. Count workdays only; one automatic repair per week; copy attributes misses to circumstances.
3. Two or three squads of 3-4, rotated monthly; weekly squad leaderboard; personal view shows only self-relative progress.
4. Weekly point budget that resets every Monday (fresh start); levels earned by hitting a modest weekly threshold.
5. All feedback informational; no prizes, no manager visibility, no link to performance review.
6. Everything opt-in with a one-click hide for streaks, points and leaderboards; no penalty for hiding.
7. One shared daily puzzle, 1-3 minutes, same for everyone, spoiler-free result card, team distribution instead of ranks.
8. Default Break menu: 5-minute easy casual game, 3-minute breathing, 10-minute walk prompt; longer breaks encouraged after heavy sessions.
9. Optional daily 1-3 minute reaction check charted against personal baseline; never compared across people.
10. No random rewards, timers, scarcity, guilt or "you'll lose" notifications; only occasional unexpected team kudos.
11. No cognitive-improvement claims anywhere in copy.

### Gaps
- No single trial tests this exact bundle in a 10-15 person workplace; the rules are a synthesis.
- Primary-source numbers for STEP UP, BE FIT, Sailer 2017, Silverman and Barasch, Rupp 2017 and Edwards 2017 could not be verified this session because the publisher domains were blocked and the search budget was exhausted.
