# Productivity and follow-through: what replicated science supports for a 1-10 minute daily "reset" ritual

Scope note for the report writer: every item below carries an evidence grade using the brief's four levels: **REPLICATED** (meta-analysis or 2+ independent RCTs), **SINGLE-STUDY**, **FAILED/MIXED REPLICATION**, or **NO EVIDENCE**. "Lab" vs "workplace/field" is stated per finding. Protocols are written as one-screen guided exercises. Research constraint: the network proxy blocked direct fetches of nearly all journal and university domains (nature.com, PMC, Springer, ScienceDirect, APA, HBR, SSRP, woopmylife.org, etc.), so figures below come from search-engine summaries of those pages plus the abstracts visible in results. Where a specific number or protocol wording could not be confirmed from a primary page, it is listed under Gaps rather than asserted.

Three headline corrections the team should know before designing anything:
1. **Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) on self-imposed deadlines was retracted in September 2026** after a failed replication and a Data Colada forensic analysis concluding the data were tampered with. Do not cite it.
2. **Brooks et al. (2016) "Don't stop believing: rituals improve performance by decreasing anxiety" was retracted in November 2024** at the authors' request over data-integrity anomalies. Do not cite it as evidence for "start rituals".
3. **Ramirez & Beilock (2011) expressive-writing-before-exams did not replicate** in the Social Sciences Replication Project; **Ward et al. (2017) smartphone "brain drain" did not replicate** in a preregistered direct replication and meta-analyses find null-to-tiny effects; **the Zeigarnik memory effect** fails in a 2025 meta-analysis (though the urge to resume an interrupted task survives).

## Key Question 1: Implementation intentions ("if-then" plans): meta-analytic effect, best format, procrastination specifically

### Takeaway
Grade: **REPLICATED** (meta-analysis of 94 studies, d = 0.65; later meta-analyses on MCII confirm smaller but robust effects). The "if [situation], then I will [action]" format outperforms a looser "global" plan in a direct comparison (n = 557), and the one study on procrastinators (Owens et al. 2008) found the plan worked regardless of procrastination level. Most evidence is lab or health-behaviour field studies, not knowledge-work output.

### Cited Findings
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38, 69-119: meta-analysis of 94 independent studies, >8,000 participants, medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = 0.65; implementation intentions helped initiating goal striving, shielding ongoing pursuit from distractions, disengaging from failing courses of action, and conserving capability for future striving. — [Univ. Konstanz publication page](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement:-A-Gollwitzer-Sheeran/c4deb3507fe725ce6363c1735f1ba83bab20d665)
- Owens, Bowman & Dill (2008), Journal of Applied Social Psychology 38, 366-384, "Overcoming procrastination: the effect of implementation intentions": participants who formed implementation intentions were nearly 8 times more likely (odds ratio) to keep their appointments than those who did not; the implementation-intention x procrastination interaction was not significant, i.e., the plan helped high and low procrastinators alike. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Overcoming-Procrastination%3A-The-Effect-of-Owens-Bowman/383297028b6836af44b70930951c16f92a30a056); [reference entry](https://www.scirp.org/(S(i43dyn45teexjx455qlt3d2q))/reference/ReferencesPapers.aspx?ReferenceID=379420)
- A student-journal conceptual replication (152 university students) reported that those who planned a time and location for an action were >40% more likely to act on it regardless of self-rated procrastination; low-quality venue, cite only as corroboration. — [Capilano College psychology journal PDF](https://cc.arcabc.ca/_flysystem/repo-bin/2020-07/cc_309.pdf)
- Chapman & Armitage (Psychology & Health), 557 participants randomised to control vs "if-then" vs "global" plan (with/without pre-instructions): fruit and vegetable intake rose by 0.50 portions in the if-then condition vs 0.31 in the global-plan condition vs 0.01 in control, so the if-then format produced a ~66% larger increase; pre-intervention instructions added nothing (argues against experimenter demand). — [PubMed 20204996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20204996/); [Psychology & Health article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08870440701864538)
- Gollwitzer's own chapter "How to maximize implementation intention effects" (Konstanz) collects the moderators of II effects (goal commitment, plan specificity, cue salience). — [Chapter PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/How-to-maximize-implementation-intention-effects.pdf)
- Bieleke, Keller & Gollwitzer (2021) "If-then planning", European Review of Social Psychology, is the current narrative review of the field. — [Review article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2020.1808936)
- Caveat on going public: Gollwitzer, Sheeran, Michalski & Seifert (2009), Psychological Science, four experiments: identity-related intentions that were noticed by others were translated into action less intensively than those that were ignored (field: one week of striving; lab), among participants strongly committed to the identity goal, because social recognition gives a premature sense of having the identity. — [Psychological Science article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02336.x); [MIT Sloan summary](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/announcing-plans-may-kill-motivation-productivity/)

### Inferences
- The if-then plan is the single best-supported micro-intervention in this whole brief for "getting started": large meta-analytic effect, strict format matters, works for procrastinators, and takes under two minutes.
- Because II effects depend on a salient cue, a reset screen should force the user to name a concrete cue (clock time + place, or "right after X") rather than "later today".
- Owens et al. shows the plan works for procrastinators but was about appointment-keeping, not creative output; treat the knowledge-work transfer as plausible, not proven.

#### Protocol (one screen, ~2 minutes): "One If-Then"
1. "Name the single task you most want to start today (one line)."
2. "Complete this sentence exactly: **If it is [time] and I am [place / right after cue], then I will [first physical action, under 5 minutes].**" (Enforce the literal if/then structure; Chapman & Armitage shows the format itself carries the effect.)
3. Optional shield: "**If [the most likely interruption/urge], then I will [specific response].**"
4. Keep it private by default; if shared with the team, phrase it as a behaviour ("I will draft the brief at 10:00"), not an identity ("I'm becoming someone who finishes"), given Gollwitzer et al. 2009.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis specific to procrastinators or knowledge-worker output was found; Owens et al. 2008 remains the main procrastination-specific experiment and its exact n/design details could not be fetched (page blocked).
- Later updates to the 2006 meta-analysis (e.g., domain-specific IIs in health) were not retrieved beyond the MCII meta-analyses in Key Question 2.

## Key Question 2: Mental contrasting / WOOP (MCII): evidence, effect sizes, protocol, replication quality

### Takeaway
Grade: **REPLICATED** (two independent meta-analyses: g = 0.336 across 15,907 participants; d = 0.36 for MCII in health behaviour). Effects are small-to-medium, smaller than for implementation intentions alone, and larger when delivered interactively (g = 0.47) than via documents (g = 0.28). Most studies are health/academic field studies; no knowledge-work productivity trials were found.

### Cited Findings
- Wang, Wang & Gai (2021), Frontiers in Psychology 12:565202: meta-analysis of 21 studies, 24 independent effect sizes, 15,907 participants; MCII effective for goal attainment with small-to-medium g = 0.336; moderated mainly by intervention style: direct participant-experimenter interaction g = 0.465 vs document-based delivery g = 0.277. — [Frontiers full text](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/text); [PMC record](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8149892/)
- Cross & Sheffield (2019), Health Psychology Review 13, 209-225: 33 studies (k = 99 effects); mental contrasting alone d = 0.325 (95% CI 0.236-0.414); MCII d = 0.359 (95% CI 0.259-0.458) for health behaviour change. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331842483_Mental_Contrasting_for_Health_Behaviour_Change_a_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-analysis_of_Effects_and_Moderator_variables)
- Abdulla & Woods (2021), School Psychology International, compared MCII against solution-focused and autonomous planning (details not retrieved; shows MCII has been tested head-to-head). — [SAGE article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01430343211000399)
- An MCII intervention to promote physical activity in working adults has been published in the European Journal of Health Psychology (workplace population; details not retrieved). — [Hogrefe article](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/full/10.1027/2512-8442/a000195)

### Inferences
- MCII is a reasonable 5-minute reset exercise with real but modest expected effects; the "interactive beats document" moderator argues for a guided, step-timed, conversational UI rather than a static form.
- The incremental value of the Wish/Outcome/Obstacle steps over a bare if-then plan is not large in these meta-analyses (MCII d = 0.36 vs II d = 0.65 in Gollwitzer & Sheeran, though populations differ); the obstacle step's main job is to generate a good "if" cue.

#### Protocol (one screen with four timed sub-steps, ~5 minutes): "WOOP the next block"
Sequence matters: mental contrasting requires imagining the positive outcome *before* the obstacle, not the reverse.
1. **Wish** (30 s): "What do you want to get done in the next [block/day]? Make it challenging but feasible. 3-6 words."
2. **Outcome** (60 s, timer on, no typing): "What is the best result of doing it? How will it feel? Close your eyes and imagine it as vividly as you can."
3. **Obstacle** (60 s): "What *inside you* most often gets in the way (a habit, an emotion, an urge)? Imagine it fully."
4. **Plan** (60 s): "**If [obstacle shows up], then I will [specific action].**" Save the plan; show it back at the start of the block.

### Gaps
- The official WOOP wording and timings on woopmylife.org (Oettingen's site) could not be fetched (domain blocked); the prompts above are paraphrases consistent with the published MCII description and should be checked against the official site before shipping.
- No MCII trial with knowledge-worker output as the dependent variable was found.
- The Wang et al. 2021 moderator for study quality/preregistration was not retrieved; the meta-analysis mixes RCTs with weaker designs.

## Key Question 3: Offloading and worry: bedtime to-do list, expressive writing before performance, Zeigarnik/open loops, "brain dump"

### Takeaway
Bedtime to-do list (Scullin 2018) is **SINGLE-STUDY** (polysomnography lab, n = 57, ~9 min faster sleep onset, specificity matters). Plan-making to close open loops (Masicampo & Baumeister 2011) is **SINGLE-STUDY** (multi-experiment paper, lab). Expressive writing immediately before a high-stakes test (Ramirez & Beilock 2011) is **FAILED/MIXED REPLICATION**. The Zeigarnik *memory* effect is **FAILED/MIXED REPLICATION** per a 2025 meta-analysis, but the *urge to resume* interrupted tasks (Ovsiankina effect) holds. "Brain dump" as a named practice has **NO EVIDENCE** beyond these mechanisms.

### Cited Findings
- Scullin, Krueger, Ballard et al. (2018), Journal of Experimental Psychology: General: 57 healthy adults spent a night in a sleep lab; half wrote for 5 minutes before bed a to-do list for the coming days, half wrote about tasks already completed; the to-do group fell asleep about 9 minutes faster on average; the more specifically participants wrote their to-do list, the faster they fell asleep, with the opposite trend for completed-activity lists. — [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/01/180111224021.htm); [Baylor summary](https://kellercenter.hankamer.baylor.edu/news/story/2018/effects-bedtime-writing); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Effects-of-Bedtime-Writing-on-Difficulty-A-and-Scullin-Krueger/432b35c7efd842bc2d73d049072336c409e2a3a6)
- Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), JPSP 101(4), 667-683, "Consider it done!": unfulfilled goals caused intrusive thoughts during unrelated tasks, high accessibility of goal-related words, and worse anagram performance; letting participants write a specific plan for the unfinished goal eliminated the intrusions and the performance cost even though the goal was still unfinished. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51234294_Consider_It_Done_Plan_Making_Can_Eliminate_the_Cognitive_Effects_of_Unfulfilled_Goals)
- Ramirez & Beilock (2011), Science: a brief expressive writing assignment immediately before an important test significantly improved exam scores, especially for habitually test-anxious students. — [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110113141605.htm)
- Social Sciences Replication Project (Camerer et al. 2018, Nature Human Behaviour): direct replications of 21 Science/Nature social-science experiments 2010-2015; 13 of 21 (62%) replicated; replication effect sizes were about half the originals. Ramirez & Beilock was one of the 21; replication authors Buttrick, Gampa, Hummer & Nosek; the original authors argued the replication failed to create a high-pressure situation because high- and low-pressure conditions did not differ, which indicates the result did not replicate. — [Nature Human Behaviour article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0399-z); [SSRP pre-registered replication report](http://www.socialsciencesreplicationproject.com/src/pre-reports/Ramirez%20and%20Beilock%20(2011)%20-%20Pre-Data%20Collection%20Replication%20Report.pdf)
- A 2024 conference study found no significant differences between expressive-writing and neutral-writing groups in academic performance or change in test anxiety, contrary to Ramirez & Beilock. — [InPACT 2024 paper](https://inpact-psychologyconference.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/202401P083.pdf)
- Shen, Yang, Zhang & Zhang (2018), PLoS ONE: 75 high-test-anxiety Chinese senior-high-school students randomised; experimental group did 20 minutes/day of positive expressive writing for 30 days vs daily-events writing; post-test anxiety significantly lower (p < 0.001). This is a 30-day programme, not a one-shot pre-exam write, so it does not test the Ramirez & Beilock paradigm. — [PLoS ONE article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0191779)
- A 2021 classroom RCT ("Does expressive writing or an instructional intervention reduce the impacts of test anxiety in a college classroom?", Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications) exists; results could not be retrieved. — [Article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-021-00309-x)
- Zeigarnik: a 1964 review concluded the effect "is far from being the invariable result... frequently, more completed than incompleted tasks are recalled"; Seifert & Patalano (1991) re-examined it. — [Seifert & Patalano 1991 PDF](https://apatalano.faculty.wesleyan.edu/files/2019/07/Seifert_Patalano_1991.pdf); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect)
- Ghibellini & Meier (2025), "Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects", Humanities & Social Sciences Communications: no memory advantage for unfinished tasks once Zeigarnik's own 1927 data are set aside, but a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks (Ovsiankina effect) holds; "the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity". — [Nature HSSC article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w)
- Morning pages / brain dump: sources acknowledge "Morning Pages as Cameron designed them haven't been directly studied in clinical trials"; support is indirect via expressive writing (Pennebaker) and cognitive offloading. — [Morning pages guide](https://blog.mylifenote.ai/morning-pages/); [Idea to Value](https://www.ideatovalue.com/crea/nickskillicorn/2021/05/julia-cameron-on-the-artists-way/)

### Inferences
- The two offloading findings that survive (Scullin; Masicampo & Baumeister) converge on one mechanism: *writing a specific next step* for each open item quiets intrusion. A generic "dump everything in your head" is not what was tested; specificity was the active ingredient in Scullin.
- The failed Ramirez & Beilock replication means a "write your worries before the big pitch" screen should not be sold as evidence-based performance enhancement; it may still be benign.
- The Ovsiankina result (urge to resume) is the best empirical basis for the "just start for 5 minutes" tactic: starting creates a pull to finish.

#### Protocol (one screen, 5 minutes): "Close the loops"
1. "List every unfinished thing on your mind (one line each, 60 s)."
2. For each: "Write the **next concrete step** and **when** you'll do it" (this is the Masicampo plan + Scullin specificity). Items with no next step get "park until [date]".
3. End with: "Tomorrow's first task is: ____ at ____." (Doubles as the bedtime to-do list if done at shutdown.)

### Gaps
- No replication of Scullin 2018 was located; it remains a single lab study with a sleep-onset (not productivity) outcome.
- The exact SSRP replication effect size and n for Ramirez & Beilock could not be retrieved (nature.com and SSRP site blocked).
- No controlled study of "brain dump" as practised in productivity culture was found.

## Key Question 4: Goal setting, progress monitoring, small wins / progress principle, daily highlights

### Takeaway
Specific, difficult goals (Locke & Latham) are **REPLICATED** (d ≈ 0.42-0.82 across meta-analyses; smaller for complex tasks). Prompting progress monitoring (Harkin et al. 2016) is **REPLICATED** (138 studies, 19,951 participants, d = 0.40 on attainment). The progress principle (Amabile & Kramer) is a large **correlational diary study** (12,000 entries), not an experiment. "Daily highlight" as a named practice has **NO EVIDENCE** beyond these.

### Cited Findings
- Locke & Latham (2002), American Psychologist, "Building a practically useful theory of goal setting": goal-difficulty effect sizes in meta-analyses d = 0.52-0.82; specific difficult goals vs "do your best" d = 0.42-0.80; effects shrink with task complexity (goal difficulty d = 0.48 for the most complex tasks vs 0.67 for the least; specific-difficult vs do-your-best d = 0.41 complex vs 0.77 simple). — [Locke & Latham 2002 PDF](https://www-2.rotman.utoronto.ca/facbios/file/09%20-%20Locke%20&%20Latham%202002%20AP.pdf)
- Harkin, Webb et al. (2016), Psychological Bulletin, "Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment?": 138 studies, 19,951 participants; interventions increased monitoring frequency (d+ = 1.98, 95% CI 1.71-2.24) and promoted goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI 0.32-0.48); authors note this is similar in size to the effect of goal intentions on attainment. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Does-monitoring-goal-progress-promote-goal-A-of-the-Harkin-Webb/71c6265bf7a8ded9084ff21a417cd63d6f4119ea); [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/598512)
- Amabile & Kramer: ~12,000 diary entries from 238 professionals in 26 project teams at 7 companies; of all factors, making progress in meaningful work was the strongest driver of a good "inner work life" (emotions, perceptions, motivation); published in The Progress Principle (2011). — [ATD summary](https://www.td.org/content/td-magazine/recognizing-meaningful-progress); [Idea to Value summary](https://www.ideatovalue.com/crea/nickskillicorn/2021/11/the-progress-principle-using-small-wins-to-ignite-joy-engagement-and-creativity-at-work/)
- Klein, Wesson, Hollenbeck & Alge (1999), Journal of Applied Psychology 84, 885-896: meta-analysis of 83 samples on goal commitment antecedents and consequences. — [PubMed 10639908](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10639908/)

### Inferences
- For creative-agency work (complex tasks), expect the lower end of goal-setting effects (d ≈ 0.4-0.5); "specific and difficult" should apply to *process* targets (e.g., "three concept directions by 12:00") rather than creative-outcome quality.
- Progress monitoring's d = 0.40 is on par with the goal itself, and it costs 30 seconds; a reset ritual that *records* yesterday's progress is one of the cheapest evidence-backed elements available.
- The progress principle supports *surfacing* small wins in a reset screen, but as a diary correlation it does not prove that logging wins causes better work.

#### Protocol (one screen, 2 minutes): "Progress ledger"
1. "Yesterday I moved forward on: ____ (one concrete thing)." (recorded, timestamped)
2. "Today's specific target: ____ by ____." (must contain a noun, a number or a deliverable, and a time)
3. Weekly: the ledger is visible to the team (see Key Question 5 on public reporting).

### Gaps
- Harkin et al.'s moderator effect sizes (physically recording progress; reporting progress publicly; monitoring frequency) could not be retrieved because the PDF and EurekAlert pages were blocked; the brief's "especially when recorded or reported publicly" claim matches the abstract as summarised in search results but the specific d values are unverified.
- No experimental test of "daily highlight" (one chosen task per day) was found.

## Key Question 5: Pre-commitment and accountability: commitment devices, stickK-style stakes, public commitment, peer effects

### Takeaway
Self-imposed costly deadlines (Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002) are now **RETRACTED** (failed 2026 replication + data-tampering finding). Financial commitment contracts are **REPLICATED in field RCTs** for health behaviour (Giné, Karlan & Zinman 2010) with low take-up. Temptation bundling is **REPLICATED** (lab-field 2014; n = 6,792 field 2020). Peer effects on productivity are supported by one strong **field study** (Mas & Moretti 2009). Public commitment raises goal commitment (Hollenbeck 1989) but announcing identity goals can *reduce* effort (Gollwitzer 2009): **MIXED**, direction depends on framing.

### Cited Findings
- Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), Psychological Science 13(3), 219-224: reported that people self-impose costly deadlines, that self-imposed deadlines improve performance, but that externally imposed evenly spaced deadlines performed best. — [MIT PDF of original](https://web.mit.edu/ariely/www/MIT/Papers/deadlines.pdf)
- Hyndman & Bisin (2026), Psychological Science 37(8): replicated Study 2 with adults at a large US public university; changes in deadlines had a negligible effect on all three performance metrics and several survey metrics; results did not replicate. — [Psychological Science article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976261460772); [SSRN preprint](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5053636)
- Data Colada analysed the original data and concluded Studies 1 and 2 had been tampered with; Retraction Watch (3 Sept 2026) reports the paper was retracted. — [Data Colada post](https://datacolada.substack.com/p/138-artificial-deadlines-part-1-evidence); [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/)
- Giné, Karlan & Zinman (2010), AEJ: Applied Economics 2(4), 213-235, CARES commitment-savings contract for smoking cessation in the Philippines: 11% of smokers offered it took up; those offered were 3 percentage points more likely to pass a 6-month nicotine test, persisting at a surprise 12-month test; treatment-on-the-treated estimates of +31 to +53 percentage points. — [AEA article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fapp.2.4.213)
- Milkman, Minson & Volpp (2014), Management Science 60(2), 283-299, "Holding the Hunger Games hostage at the gym": gym-only access to tempting audiobooks raised gym visits 51% (full treatment) and 29% (intermediate) vs control initially; effects decayed over time (especially after Thanksgiving); 61% of participants afterwards opted to pay for gym-only audiobook access. — [PubMed 25843979](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25843979/); [Milkman PDF](https://katherinemilkman.squarespace.com/s/holding-the-hunger-games-hostage-at-the-gym-35pa.pdf)
- Kirgios et al. (2020), OBHDP 161, 20-35, "Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise": n = 6,792 in a 4-week programme; audiobook + bundling encouragement raised the likelihood of a weekly gym visit 10-14% during and up to 17 weeks after; encouragement alone +4% during and +6% in the 10 weeks after; roughly 15x larger than prior work. — [ScienceDirect article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X)
- Mas & Moretti (2009), American Economic Review 99(1), "Peers at work": 394 cashiers in 6 stores of a national supermarket chain, high-frequency scanner data; introducing a single highly productive worker into a shift raised the group's speed by about 1%; top performers scanned ~30% more than the slowest; mechanism was being observed by the faster worker (peer pressure), and optimal shift mixing could have saved ~123,529 labour hours (~$2.5M) per year. — [AEA article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.99.1.112); [Wilson Quarterly summary](https://www.wilsonquarterly.com/quarterly/summer-2009-thrift-the-double-edged-virtue/speedy-spillovers)
- Hollenbeck, Williams & Klein (1989), Journal of Applied Psychology 74, 18-23: committing to goals publicly rather than privately enhanced goal commitment. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232503569_An_Empirical_Examination_of_the_Antecedents_of_Commitment_to_Difficult_Goals)
- Gollwitzer et al. (2009): identity-related intentions noticed by others were acted on less intensively (see Key Question 1). — [Psychological Science article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02336.x)
- Fresh start effect: Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014), Management Science 60(10), 2563-2582: three archival field studies: Google searches for "diet", university gym visits and goal commitments rose at the start of weeks, months, years and after holidays. — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf); Dai, Milkman & Riis (2015), Psychological Science: causal evidence that emphasising a temporal landmark increases intentions to initiate goal pursuit. — [Psychological Science article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797615605818)
- Beshears, Dai, Milkman & Benartzi (2021), OBHDP 167, 72-87: field experiment with 6,082 university employees; framing a future contribution-increase date as a fresh start (birthday, first day of spring) increased choice of that date without reducing immediate increases, and raised retirement contributions over the following 8 months; first consequential field demonstration. — [Milkman PDF](https://katherinemilkman.squarespace.com/s/using-fresh-starts-to-nudge-increased-retirement-savings.pdf)

### Inferences
- With Ariely & Wertenbroch gone, the evidence for "set yourself a deadline with a penalty" rests on health-behaviour commitment contracts: real but small intention-to-treat effects, low take-up, and bigger effects only among the self-selected who opt in. Offer stakes as an opt-in, never a default.
- Mas & Moretti implies visible, concurrent work (a shared "working now on X" board) can lift output modestly through observation; it is the best field evidence for lightweight social accountability. It was measured on routine, countable work (scanning), so transfer to creative work is unproven.
- Public commitments should be about *behaviour* and *progress reports* (Hollenbeck; Harkin), not identity claims (Gollwitzer 2009).
- Fresh-start framing is cheap: label Monday and the 1st of the month as "reset" days in the UI.

#### Protocol (one screen, 1-2 minutes): "Commit out loud (optional stakes)"
1. "What will you finish by when? '**I will [deliverable] by [time].**'" posted to the team board (behaviour, not identity).
2. Optional: "Add a stake? [none / coffee for the team / donate X]" (opt-in; expect low take-up).
3. On Monday/1st of month the screen says "Fresh start: last week is closed. What's the first move?"

### Gaps
- No RCT of stickK-style stakes for knowledge-work deliverables was found; the NBER exercise-commitment RCT ("Nudges in exercise commitment contracts") exists but details were not retrieved. — [NBER w21406](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21406/w21406.pdf)
- The widely quoted "65% with an accountability partner, 95% with a scheduled check-in (ASTD)" statistic could not be traced to any primary source in this session; treat as unsourced.

## Key Question 6: Starting: "5-minute rule", task initiation, "eat the frog", chronotype/peak timing, timeboxing vs to-do lists

### Takeaway
"Eat the frog" has **NO direct productivity evidence**; the only experimental work (Schroeder et al. 2020, six experiments, n = 2,013) shows hard-first ordering *builds self-efficacy* while people wrongly prefer easy-first. Chronotype "synchrony effect" is **MIXED**: >80% of studies show no main effect of chronotype and a 2023 latent-variable study found no general synchrony boost, though ~45% of adult studies find it for attention/inhibition/memory. Timeboxing vs to-do lists: **NO controlled evidence**; the mechanism (implementation intentions) is the real support. The "5-minute rule" is best supported indirectly by the Ovsiankina resumption effect and by implementation intentions.

### Cited Findings
- Schroeder et al. (2020), Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, "To build efficacy, eat the frog first": six experiments, 2,013 participants; people believed increasing-difficulty order would enhance efficacy and preferred it, but completing tasks in increasing-difficulty order actually reduced self-efficacy relative to hard-first or random order. — [ScienceDirect article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103120303723); [author PDF](https://juliana-schroeder-dev.squarespace.com/s/Eat-The-Frog-First.pdf)
- Chronotype systematic review (Chronobiology International, 2025): >80% of reviewed studies showed no main effect of chronotype on cognition; 29 of 64 (45%) adult (18-45) studies showed a synchrony effect, mostly in attention, inhibition and memory; 10 of 12 (83%) studies in older adults showed it. — [Chronobiology International review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07420528.2025.2490495)
- Collabra: Psychology (2023), "The interplay of time-of-day and chronotype results in no general and robust cognitive boost": at the latent-variable level no robust, general synchrony effect; some domains benefited at "non-optimal" times. — [Collabra article](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/9/1/88337/197502/The-Interplay-of-Time-of-day-and-Chronotype)
- May, Hasher & Healey (2023), Perspectives on Psychological Science, "For whom (and when) the time bell tolls": the founding authors' review of the synchrony effect. — [Perspectives article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17456916231178553)
- Hunter & Wu (2016), Journal of Applied Psychology, "Give me a better break": ~1,000 breaks analysed; breaks taken earlier in the shift and spent on preferred activities produced more resource recovery; longer and more frequent short breaks beat infrequent short breaks; morning 5-15 minute breaks better restored energy and reduced afternoon health symptoms. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Give-me-a-better-break:-Choosing-workday-break-to-Hunter-Wu/eac12e8544efff04a9697e1e0b47f017eee8fb53); [APA Monitor summary](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2019/01/break)
- Timeboxing: the HBR claim that timeboxing is "the most useful" of 100 productivity hacks comes from a survey/synthesis, not a controlled trial. — [HBR 2018](https://hbr.org/2018/12/how-timeboxing-works-and-why-it-will-make-you-more-productive)
- Two-minute rule: originates in David Allen's Getting Things Done (2001); sources offer only indirect support from habit research on lowering activation energy. — [GTD site](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2020/05/the-two-minute-rule-2/)
- Ovsiankina effect (urge to resume interrupted tasks) is supported as "a general tendency" in the 2025 meta-analysis. — [Nature HSSC meta-analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w)

### Inferences
- Do not present "eat the frog" as evidence-based for output; what can be said is that doing a hard task first does not harm efficacy and may build it (lab, self-report outcome).
- Chronotype-based scheduling is a weak lever for the average adult; a simple "protect your first focused block of the day" rule is defensible on the Hunter & Wu finding that resources are highest early, not on synchrony effects.
- Timeboxing is best justified as "an implementation intention on a calendar"; frame it that way and cite Gollwitzer, not the HBR survey.
- The "start for 5 minutes" tactic combines two supported mechanisms: an if-then plan with a tiny first action, and the resumption pull once started.

#### Protocol (one screen, 1 minute): "First five"
1. "What is the smallest physical action that starts [task]? (open the file, write the headline, name the layers)."
2. "Start a 5-minute timer. When it ends you may stop." (Design note: most will continue; the Ovsiankina pull is the mechanism.)
3. Place the block on the calendar with a time, which converts it into an if-then plan.

### Gaps
- No experiment comparing timeboxing against to-do lists on output was found.
- No study of the "two-minute rule" or of "power hours" was found.
- The brief's "circadian peak performance" question: beyond the synchrony-effect reviews, no workplace study linking scheduling of hard tasks to chronotype and output was retrieved.

## Key Question 7: Focus: attention residue, task-switching costs, interruptions, monotasking, phone presence, notification batching, email frequency

### Takeaway
Attention residue (Leroy 2009) and the "ready-to-resume plan" fix (Leroy & Glomb 2018) are **SINGLE-LAB-GROUP** findings (multiple experiments, one group; no independent replication found). Interruption costs are real but the famous "23 minutes 15 seconds" and "40% of productive time" figures are **misquotes**: the 2005 field study reports ~25 minutes before return with 2.3 intervening tasks; the "40%" is an APA press paraphrase of a lab switch cost measured in ~1 second units. Phone "brain drain" is **FAILED/MIXED REPLICATION** (null preregistered replication; meta-analytic d ≈ -0.02 to -0.14, only working memory possibly affected). Notification batching (Fitz et al. 2019, n = 237) and 3x/day email checking (Kushlev & Dunn 2015, n = 124) are each **SINGLE-STUDY field experiments** with consistent directions. Heavy media multitasking is associated with *worse*, not better, cognitive control (**REPLICATED association, small effects**).

### Cited Findings
- Leroy (2009), OBHDP, "Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks": when people moved from an unfinished task, part of their attention stayed on it, harming performance on the next task. — [Chief Learning Officer summary](https://www.chieflearningofficer.com/2018/03/02/conquering-attention-residue/)
- Leroy & Glomb (2018), Organization Science 29(3), 380-397, "Tasks interrupted": anticipating time pressure on resumption caused attention residue and poor performance on the interrupting task; a brief "ready-to-resume" plan written before switching significantly reduced attention residue and improved performance on the interrupting task; Leroy: the plan "doesn't have to be very elaborate, just enough for your brain to know, 'I know how to resume this task, I'll remember, now I can let go.'" — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v29y2018i3p380-397.html); [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2018/01/16/task-interrupted-a-plan-for-returning-helps-you-move-on/)
- Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005), CHI, "No task left behind?": observed information workers; 77% of interrupted tasks were resumed the same day but it took ~25 minutes on average and 2.3 intervening tasks to get back; work was fragmented into short "working spheres". — [Summary](https://www.highperformanceroutines.com/no-task-left-behind-examining-the-nature-of-fragmented-work/); [Manchester record](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/no-task-left-behind-examining-the-name-of-fragmented-work/)
- Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008), CHI, "The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress": lab experiment with an email-answering task under interruption; interrupted workers compensated by working faster but reported higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. The "23 minutes 15 seconds" figure is reported in Mark's communications about her field work. — [ACM record](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1357054.1357072); [PDF](https://interruptions.net/literature/Mark-CHI08.pdf); critique of the misquotes: [Tab Extension blog](https://thetabextension.com/blog/cost-of-context-switching); [Productivity Report](https://productivityreport.org/2025/04/11/how-much-time-do-we-lose-task-switching/)
- Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans (2001): four lab experiments on task switching; representative switch cost ~1,100 ms on arithmetic tasks, rising with rule complexity and falling with a cue; the "40% of productive time" line is the APA's paraphrase quoting Meyer, not a result in the paper. — [APA "Multitasking: switching costs"](https://www.apa.org/topics/research/multitasking); [Tab Extension analysis](https://thetabextension.com/blog/cost-of-context-switching)
- Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017), JACR, "Brain drain": proposed that the mere presence of one's own smartphone occupies limited-capacity cognitive resources. — [FORRT replication atlas entry](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1086/691462/)
- Ruiz Pardo & Minda (2022), Acta Psychologica, preregistered direct replication: no evidence that smartphone presence or proximity (desk, pocket/bag, outside room) impaired working memory (OSPAN) or inhibitory control (go/no-go). — [ScienceDirect article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691822002323)
- Meta-analyses: "Does the brain drain effect really exist?" (2023): pooled g = -0.14 (95% CI -0.24 to -0.03). — [PMC10525686](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10525686/); Media Psychology (2023) meta-analysis of 56 studies, 7,093 participants: only working-memory capacity showed a significant pooled effect, other cognitive functions null. — [Media Psychology article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15213269.2023.2286647); four-level meta-analysis (166 effects, 53 samples, 33 studies): d = -0.02, no significant effect. — [TMB PDF](https://tmb.apaopen.org/pub/7np97zr5/download/pdf); one 2023 study did find reduced basal attention with phone present. — [Scientific Reports 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-36256-4)
- Fitz, Kushlev et al. (2019), Computers in Human Behavior 101, 84-94: randomised field experiment, n = 237 (India), two weeks; notifications delivered as usual vs batched vs never; batching three times a day reduced stress, improved concentration, and participants felt more productive, more in control and in better mood vs as-they-occur. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Batching-smartphone-notifications-can-improve-Fitz-Kushlev/ce19cf66bdc6ced56a2f16d2483d97f9dc59b21d); [Prosocial Design summary](https://www.prosocialdesign.org/library/batching-notifications)
- Kushlev & Dunn (2015), Computers in Human Behavior 43, 220-228: 124 adults, within-person crossover over two weeks; limiting email checks to three times a day for one week reduced daily stress vs unlimited checking, and lowered tension during an important activity; mechanism offered: email "fragments our attention". — [Paper PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Kushlev-ComputHumBehav15.pdf); [UBC summary](https://psych.ubc.ca/news/checking-your-email-less-can-help-reduce-stress-new-study-by-phd-candidate-kostadin-kushlev/)
- Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009), PNAS: heavy media multitaskers were more susceptible to interference from irrelevant stimuli and memory representations and performed worse on task switching. — [PubMed 19706386](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19706386/)
- Uncapher & Wagner (2018) review of 21 reports: "the literature is still sparse... the balance of evidence suggests that heavier media multitaskers exhibit poorer performance in a number of cognitive domains". — [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2018/10/decade-data-reveals-heavy-multitaskers-reduced-memory-psychologist-says)
- Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein (2017), Attention, Perception & Psychophysics: two replications plus meta-analysis of the Ophir effect (small/inconsistent); and a 2021 "ten years on" meta-analysis. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-017-1408-4); [Cyberpsychology meta-analysis](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/view/13303)

### Inferences
- The most implementable focus finding is Leroy & Glomb's ready-to-resume plan: a 30-60 second note at every forced switch. It is single-group evidence, so present it as "promising", but it is cheap and mechanistically consistent with Masicampo & Baumeister.
- The agency's design copy should not say "23 minutes to refocus" or "multitasking costs 40%"; say "field observation: ~25 minutes and 2-3 other tasks before people get back to an interrupted task".
- Phone-in-another-room rules are not supported as cognitive enhancers; batching notifications and scheduling email checks (3x/day) have better, if single-study, support and target stress rather than raw output.
- Monotasking is supported mainly by the switch-cost literature (lab, sub-second costs that compound) and by the absence of any evidence that habitual multitaskers get better at it.

#### Protocol (one screen, 60 seconds, triggered on any switch or break): "Ready-to-resume"
1. "Where exactly am I in [task]? (one line)"
2. "The next concrete step when I come back is: ____"
3. "Anything I must not forget: ____"
4. Then: "Notifications are batched until [next batch time]. Email next at [10:00 / 13:00 / 16:00]."

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and exact effect sizes for Leroy (2009) and Leroy & Glomb (2018) could not be retrieved (pages blocked); no independent replication was found.
- The exact source of "23 minutes 15 seconds" (which Mark publication/interview) was not pinned down; the 2005 paper's figure as summarised is ~25 minutes.
- Fitz et al. 2019's "never" condition results (reported elsewhere as increased anxiety/FOMO) were not confirmed here.

## Key Question 8: Breaks: scheduled vs self-initiated, the "4 hours of deliberate practice" claim, focused-session length, planned breaks and errors, Pomodoro's status

### Takeaway
Micro-breaks are **REPLICATED for vigor/fatigue (d ≈ 0.35) but not for performance (d = 0.16, ns)** (Albulescu et al. 2022 meta-analysis, N = 2,335). Brief diversions preventing vigilance decrement (Ariga & Lleras 2011) is **SINGLE-STUDY** (lab). Systematic vs self-regulated breaks (Biwer et al. 2023, n = 87) shows **no learning difference**, but systematic breaks gave better mood, less fatigue and similar completion in less time: **SINGLE-STUDY** with at least two later studies (2025, 2026) whose results could not be retrieved. Ericsson's "about 4 hours a day" ceiling is an observational claim from the 1993 violinist study, and deliberate practice explains only ~14-19% of performance variance in the Macnamara et al. 2014 meta-analysis: treat the 4-hour rule as **SINGLE-STUDY, observational**. Pomodoro's specific 25/5 parameters have **never been directly tested**.

### Cited Findings
- Albulescu et al. (2022), PLoS ONE 17(8): e0272460, "Give me a break!": 19 records, 22 samples, N = 2,335; micro-breaks increased vigor (d = 0.36, p < .001; k = 9, n = 913), reduced fatigue (d = 0.35, p < .001; k = 9, n = 803), but had a non-significant effect on overall performance (d = 0.16, p = .116; k = 15, n = 1,132); performance effects significant only for less cognitively demanding tasks; longer breaks gave larger performance boosts. — [PLoS ONE PDF](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0272460&type=printable); [PMC record](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Ariga & Lleras (2011), Cognition, "Brief and rare mental 'breaks' keep you focused": over a 50-minute task, the control group's accuracy declined steadily while a group given two brief diversions maintained accuracy throughout; authors' account: goal deactivation/reactivation, analogous to sensory habituation. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/02/110208131529.htm); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222820913_Brief_and_rare_mental_breaks_keep_you_focused_Deactivation_and_reactivation_of_task_goals_preempt_vigilance_decrements)
- Biwer et al. (2023), British Journal of Educational Psychology 93(2), 353-367, "Understanding effort regulation: comparing 'Pomodoro' breaks and self-regulated breaks": self-regulated (n = 35) vs systematic-long (6-min break every 24 min, n = 25) vs systematic-short (3-min break every 12 min, n = 27); no significant performance difference; self-regulated studiers took longer sessions and breaks and reported more fatigue and distraction and lower concentration and motivation; systematic breaks had mood benefits and apparent efficiency benefits (similar completion in shorter time). — [Wiley article](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjep.12593); [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel/)
- Follow-ups exist: "Investigating the effectiveness of self-regulated, Pomodoro, and Flowtime break-taking techniques among students" (2025) and "When to take a break: comparing effects of systematic short (Pomodoro) and self-regulated breaks on subjective experience and actual learning" (Behavioral Sciences 2026); results not retrieved. — [PubMed 40723645](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40723645/); [Behavioral Sciences 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16071158)
- Hunter & Wu (2016): preferred activities, earlier-in-day breaks, longer breaks and frequent short breaks all associated with greater recovery (see Key Question 6). — [APA Monitor summary](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2019/01/break)
- Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), Psychological Review 100(3), 363-406: reported "essentially no benefit from durations exceeding 4 hr per day and reduced benefits from practice exceeding 2 hr", and that effective deliberate practice may be closer to 1 hr/day; the best violinists practised alone ~3.5 h/day vs 1.3 h/day for music-teacher trainees; 4 h/day proposed as a general sustainable limit. — [Matuschak notes on the paper](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zEkCRJXM9NYCXxzFoDaNhL)
- Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), Psychological Science, meta-analysis of 157 effect sizes, N > 11,000: deliberate practice explained a corrected 14% (95% CI 11-18%) of performance variance overall (figures of 19% and "about one-third of reliable variance" appear under different corrections). — [Macnamara et al. 2014 PDF](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/Macnamara-et-al.-2014.pdf); Macnamara & Maitra (2019) attempted to replicate the 1993 violinist study. — [PMC6731745](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6731745/)

### Inferences
- The honest claim for breaks is "they restore energy and mood; they do not reliably raise output on demanding work in the short term". For a creative agency that is still valuable (fatigue and distraction predict errors), but the Reset area should not promise productivity gains from breaks alone.
- Biwer et al. supports *scheduled* breaks over "take one when you feel like it", because self-regulated breakers over-run and feel worse; the specific 25/5 is untested, 24/6 and 12/3 both worked.
- Ariga & Lleras supports inserting *brief* diversions (seconds to a minute) during long vigilance work such as QA or retouching passes.
- Ericsson's 4-hour ceiling is a reasonable planning heuristic (two focused blocks of ~90-120 min) but it is observational, from musicians, and the deliberate-practice literature has been heavily contested.

#### Protocol (timer screen): "Scheduled break"
1. Default cadence selectable: 25/5 or 50/10 (both inside the range tested); break time is auto-scheduled, not left to feel.
2. Break content prompt: "Pick something you *like* (walk, music, chat), and take your longer break before lunch." (Hunter & Wu)
3. Before the break fires: the Ready-to-resume note (Key Question 7).
4. Hard stop suggestion: after ~4 hours of deep work in a day, switch to shallow tasks.

### Gaps
- Results of the 2025 and 2026 Pomodoro/Flowtime comparisons could not be retrieved (PubMed/MDPI blocked).
- Ariga & Lleras sample size and the exact diversion length were not confirmed from the primary page.
- No workplace RCT of Pomodoro on knowledge-worker output was found.

## Key Question 9: Rituals and warm-ups: pre-performance routines, start-of-day planning, shutdown ritual, end-of-day reflection

### Takeaway
Brooks et al. (2016) "rituals reduce anxiety and improve performance" is **RETRACTED**. The surviving evidence for pre-performance routines comes from **sport (REPLICATED: 112 effect sizes; g ≈ 0.64-0.70 in experiments)**. End-of-day reflection (Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano & Staats) is a **SINGLE field experiment + lab studies** (23% better training-test scores from 15 minutes of reflection); no independent replication found. "Shutdown ritual" (Newport) has **NO direct evidence**; it inherits support from plan-making (Masicampo & Baumeister), bedtime lists (Scullin) and the psychological-detachment literature. Fixed start-of-day planning has **NO direct trial** but is a bundle of supported elements (if-then plan, progress record, specific goal).

### Cited Findings
- Brooks, Schroeder, Risen, Gino, Galinsky, Norton & Schweitzer (2016), OBHDP 137, 71-85: retracted in November 2024 at the authors' request because they "no longer [had] confidence in the integrity of the data"; the notice cites "streaks of heart rate ratings that were unlikely to have occurred naturally" and anomalies in Study 1b. — [Retraction notice (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597824000694); [Retracted article page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959781630437X); [Skeptical Inquirer commentary](https://skepticalinquirer.org/exclusive/maybe-do-stop-believing-in-rituals/)
- Hobson et al. (2017), "Rituals decrease the neural response to performance failure" (PeerJ; ERP study) is a separate, non-retracted line of ritual evidence; details not retrieved. — [PMC5452956](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5452956/)
- Rupprecht et al. (2021), International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology, meta-analysis of pre-performance routines: 112 effect sizes; pre-post designs small effect (SMC = 0.31, 95% CI 0.18-0.44); experimental designs moderate-to-large under low pressure (g = 0.64, 95% CI 0.45-0.83) and under pressure (g = 0.70, 95% CI 0.24-1.16); not moderated by routine type, age, gender, skill level or intervention characteristics. — [Taylor & Francis article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1944271)
- Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano & Staats, "Making experience count / Learning by thinking": mixed-method (lab + field); field experiment at an international call centre (technology support for a Western client) during a two-week training programme with control, reflection (15-minute journal prompt on lessons learned at the end of each day) and sharing (10 min journal + 5 min discussion) groups; reflection group scored 23% better on the final training test than those who used the time for additional practice; lab studies showed ~21% performance gains from reflecting instead of practising; mechanisms proposed: task understanding and self-efficacy; HEC working paper 2016, Management Science version 2023. — [HEC working paper record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ebg/heccah/1181.html); [SSRN](https://dx.doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2414478); [HBS Working Knowledge summary](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/reflecting-on-work-improves-job-performance); [HBR 2014 summary](https://hbr.org/2014/04/to-enhance-your-learning-take-a-few-minutes-to-think-about-what-youve-learned)
- Psychological detachment: Sonnentag's recovery research shows detachment (mentally disconnecting from work in off-job time) is most strongly related to fatigue, exhaustion, well-being and sleep; high workload reduces detachment (the "recovery paradox"); end-of-workday negative affect links morning workload to poorer evening detachment. — [Sonnentag recovery paradox PDF](https://www.helsinki.fi/assets/drupal/2024-04/Sonnentag.pdf); [Recovery from work-related effort meta-analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319067520_Recovery_from_work-related_effort_A_meta-analysis); [Annual Review of Organizational Psychology](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-012420-091355)

### Inferences
- A "ready to start" ritual can be defended from sport evidence (routines of any type help, especially under pressure) but the mechanism in the agency context (pitches, presentations) is an extrapolation; the one organizational study is gone.
- The shutdown ritual should be built from the three supported parts: (1) specific next steps for every open item, (2) tomorrow's list, (3) a 2-5 minute "what did I learn" reflection. Newport's name for it is fine; cite the components, not the book.
- The Di Stefano effect was measured on training-test scores for new call-centre hires; the 23% is not a general productivity gain.

#### Protocol (one screen, 5-7 minutes): "Shutdown"
1. "Open loops: for each, write the next step and when." (2-3 min)
2. "Tomorrow's first task at [time] is ____." (30 s)
3. "Reflect (2 min): What did I learn today? What would I do differently next time? What is one thing that went well?" (Di Stefano prompt family; keep it to lessons, not venting.)
4. Close with a fixed phrase/button ("Shutdown complete") as the routine cue; detachment research argues the ritual's job is to let the mind leave work.

#### Protocol (one screen, 60-90 seconds): "Pre-pitch routine"
A fixed, personal, repeatable sequence (e.g., 3 slow breaths, say the opening line once, check the first slide) performed identically each time; type does not matter per the sport meta-analysis, consistency does.

### Gaps
- No independent replication of Di Stefano et al. was found; co-author data-integrity controversies at HBS (2023) are widely reported but were not verified in this session and should be checked before relying on the paper.
- No controlled trial of a "shutdown ritual" or "start-of-day planning routine" as a package was found.
- Hobson et al. 2017 details (n, effect size) were not retrieved.

## Key Question 10: Energy: sleep, caffeine timing, hydration, meal timing / post-lunch dip, light exposure

### Takeaway
Sleep is the biggest lever and is **REPLICATED** (Van Dongen 2003 dose-response; Lim & Dinges 2010 meta-analysis: overall g = -0.56, sustained attention up to d = -1.33). Caffeine 40-300 mg reliably improves attention/vigilance/reaction time (**REPLICATED**; timing-to-sleep guidance not retrieved). Mild dehydration (~1.5% body mass) impairs mood and attention (**REPLICATED**: meta-analysis of 33 studies, overall d = -0.21, attention -0.52), but most studies induce dehydration by exercise/heat, not office sitting. The post-lunch dip is mainly **circadian**, modulated by meal size and prior sleep. Daytime bright light has **MIXED** effects on alertness (positive in morning/sleep-restricted studies; null for 5 h afternoon exposure at 1,200 lx).

### Cited Findings
- Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003), Sleep 26(2), 117-128: participants randomised to 4, 6 or 8 h time in bed for 14 consecutive nights; 4 h and 6 h produced significant cumulative, dose-dependent deficits on all cognitive tasks; after 14 days the 4 h and 6 h groups performed like people after 1-2 nights of total sleep deprivation, while subjective sleepiness levelled off (people underestimated their impairment). — [UPenn PDF](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/VanDongen2003CumulativeCost.pdf)
- Lim & Dinges (2010), Psychological Bulletin 136(3), 375-389: meta-analysis of 147 cognitive tests from 70 articles on short-term sleep deprivation; overall effect g = -0.564 (95% CI -0.406 to -0.722); lapses in simple attention g = -0.776 (95% CI -0.96 to -0.60); vigilance/sustained attention most affected (d = -1.33); reasoning accuracy least (g = -0.125, ns). — [UPenn PDF](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/LimDinges2010MetaAnalysis.pdf)
- McLellan, Caldwell & Lieberman (2016), Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews: caffeine doses of ~32-300 mg (0.5-4 mg/kg) enhance attention, vigilance and reaction time. — [ScienceDirect review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763416300690); a 2025 meta-analysis (Psychopharmacology) of caffeine's acute effect on attention found doses >= 200 mg improved reaction time and accuracy more than lower doses, with a linear dose-response for reaction time and a quadratic (inverted-U) one for accuracy. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00213-025-06775-1); an RCT on dose and timing of caffeine and subsequent sleep exists (details not retrieved). — [PMC11985402](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985402/)
- Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford (2018), Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, "Dehydration impairs cognitive performance: a meta-analysis": 33 studies, 280 effect sizes, 413 subjects, 1-6% body-mass loss; overall ES = -0.21 (95% CI -0.31 to -0.11); attention -0.52, executive function -0.24, motor coordination -0.40; >2% body-mass loss ES = -0.28 vs <= 2% ES = -0.14 (p = 0.04). — [MSSE article](https://journals.lww.com/acsm-msse/Fulltext/2018/11000/Dehydration_Impairs_Cognitive_Performance__A.21.aspx)
- Ganio et al. (2011), British Journal of Nutrition (26 men): 1.6% body-mass loss reduced visual vigilance and working memory and increased fatigue and anxiety; Armstrong et al. (2012), Journal of Nutrition (25 women): 1.4% loss worsened vigor, fatigue, total mood disturbance, concentration difficulty and headaches. — [Armstrong 2012](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)02889-9/fulltext); [Ganio 2011](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51474586_Mild_dehydration_impairs_cognitive_performance_and_mood_of_men); [Hydration for Health summary](https://www.hydrationforhealth.com/en/hydration-science/emerging-science/list-emerging-science-article/laurence-e-armstrong-2012-matthew-s-ganio-2011/)
- Post-lunch dip: Monk (2005), "The post-lunch dip in performance": evidenced in lab tests of reaction time, alertness and sleepiness; "a bi-circadian phenomenon, largely unrelated to lunch, and worsened by a disturbed prior night's sleep"; depends on person, meal size and task. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7848298_The_Post-Lunch_Dip_in_Performance); meal size increased post-lunch sleepiness during monotonous driving. — [Physiology & Behavior 2012](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031938411005555); an afternoon nap and bright light both improved post-lunch cognitive flexibility. — [PLoS ONE 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0125359)
- Light: repeated morning bright white light in a simulated office (n = 56) improved reaction speed on simple attention tasks with higher heart-rate variability and no habituation. — [Scientific Reports 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-35689-1); 5 h of 1,200 lx vs 200 lx in the afternoon: no significant differences in sleepiness or psychomotor vigilance. — [PMC8691749](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8691749/); bright light did not prevent alertness decline from a high-cognitive-load task (n = 20, crossover). — [Journal of Environmental Psychology 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272494417300403); short-wavelength-enriched daytime light improved cognitive performance in sleep-restricted young adults. — [PMC7937889](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7937889/)

### Inferences
- Nothing in the reset ritual will outweigh a chronic 6-hour sleep schedule; the Van Dongen finding that people stop noticing their own impairment justifies a non-judgemental "hours slept last night" check-in that nudges task choice (shallow vs deep) rather than lecturing.
- Hydration effects are small and mostly from exercise/heat-induced dehydration; a "drink water" nudge is cheap and harmless but should not be oversold.
- The post-lunch dip is circadian: schedule low-stakes or collaborative work 13:00-15:00, keep lunch moderate, and consider a short nap or outdoor light rather than fighting it with hard creative work.
- Morning light is better supported than afternoon light; "step outside in the first hour" is the defensible nudge.

#### Protocol (one screen, 45 seconds): "Energy check"
1. "Hours slept: [slider]." If < 6: "Plan shallow/collaborative work for your hardest hour; protect tomorrow's sleep."
2. "Water nearby? Caffeine (if any) now, ideally not within ~6-8 h of bedtime." (timing guidance flagged below as not verified in this session)
3. "Had daylight yet today? If not, 10 minutes outside before your first deep block."
4. "Post-lunch (13:00-15:00): book meetings/admin, not your hardest creative work."

### Gaps
- Specific caffeine-timing evidence (e.g., minimum hours before bed) was not retrieved; the RCT above exists but its numbers were not accessible.
- No office-based (sedentary) hydration RCT with productivity outcomes was found; dehydration studies mostly use exercise or heat.
- Meal composition effects (high-carbohydrate lunch and attention in over-40s) were seen only in a search summary without a pinned primary source.

## Key Question 11: Gratitude ("three good things"), savouring, best-possible-self and workplace outcomes

### Takeaway
"Three good things" is **REPLICATED for well-being but with small effects**: Seligman et al. 2005 showed gains lasting six months; Davis et al. 2016 meta-analysis finds d = 0.17 on psychological well-being vs an alternative activity and d ≈ -0.03 vs psychologically active controls. Best-possible-self is **REPLICATED** with somewhat larger effects (d ≈ 0.33 well-being, 0.51 positive affect; Carrillo et al. 2019, 29 studies). **No evidence** links either to workplace productivity; savouring was not covered in retrieved sources.

### Cited Findings
- Seligman, Steen, Park & Peterson (2005), American Psychologist 60(5), 410-421: online RCT of positive interventions; writing three good things that happened each day (and why) for one week increased happiness and decreased depressive symptoms, with effects at one week, one month, three months and six months, especially among those who kept doing it. — [Paper PDF (Greater Good)](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Seligman-PosPsychProgress.pdf)
- Davis et al. (2016), Journal of Counseling Psychology, "Thankful for the little things": gratitude interventions had limited effects: d = 0.31 vs measurement-only/waitlist controls, d = -0.03 vs psychologically active comparisons; vs alternative-activity controls, gratitude d = 0.46 (k = 15), psychological well-being d = 0.17 (95% CI 0.09-0.24; k = 20), anxiety d = 0.11 (ns; k = 5). — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/284163997_Thankful_for_the_Little_Things_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Gratitude_Interventions)
- Cregg & Cheavens (2021), "Gratitude interventions: effective self-help? A meta-analysis of the impact on symptoms of depression and anxiety" (small effects; numbers not retrieved). — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339431121_Gratitude_Interventions_Effective_Self-help_A_Meta-analysis_of_the_Impact_on_Symptoms_of_Depression_and_Anxiety); a 2025 PNAS cross-cultural meta-analysis of gratitude interventions on well-being exists (numbers not retrieved). — [PNAS article](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2425193122)
- Carrillo et al. (2019), PLoS ONE, best-possible-self meta-analysis: 29 studies (26 articles), 2,909 participants; well-being d+ = 0.325, optimism d+ = 0.334, positive affect d+ = 0.511, negative affect d+ = 0.192, depressive symptoms d+ = 0.115; BPS beat gratitude interventions on positive affect (d+ = 0.326) and negative affect (d+ = 0.485); no significant moderators except a trend for age and total minutes of practice. — [PLoS ONE article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0222386)

### Inferences
- If the team wants a positive-affect element in the reset, best-possible-self (imagine and write about your best possible future in a domain, 5 min) has better meta-analytic numbers than three good things and the same cost.
- Neither should be marketed as productivity tools; they are well-being tools with small effects, and effects shrink toward zero against active controls.

#### Protocol (optional, 3-5 minutes, end of week): "Best possible week"
"Imagine the end of next week went as well as it realistically could. Write what happened, in the present tense, for 3 minutes." (Keep three-good-things as an alternate; its evidence is weaker but non-zero.)

### Gaps
- No RCT of gratitude or BPS with job performance or creative output as the outcome was found.
- Savouring interventions were not covered by any retrieved source.
- Workplace gratitude trials among healthcare workers (Sexton & Adair) are known to exist but could not be searched (budget exhausted).

## Key Question 12: Procrastination-specific: self-compassion, self-forgiveness, temporal discounting, 2-minute rule, temptation bundling, fresh start

### Takeaway
Self-compassion and self-forgiveness were originally **correlational** (Sirois 2014; Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett 2010), but a 2025 RCT (n = 459, 14-day online self-compassion programme) reduced bedtime procrastination: now **SINGLE-RCT plus correlational**. Temptation bundling is **REPLICATED** (two field experiments, the second with n = 6,792). Fresh-start framing is **REPLICATED** (archival 2014; lab 2015; field RCT n = 6,082 in 2021). The 2-minute rule has **NO EVIDENCE**. Temporal-discounting evidence was not retrieved in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Sirois (2014), Self and Identity 13(2), 128-145, "Procrastination and stress: exploring the role of self-compassion": self-compassion partially mediated the procrastination-stress relationship across samples; author suggests interventions raising self-compassion may benefit procrastinators by reducing the negative states that drive mood-repair avoidance. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/91791/1/ProcrastinationFINAL.pdf)
- Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett (2010), Personality and Individual Differences 48(7), 803-808, "I forgive myself, now I can study": students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before a first mid-term procrastinated less before the next exam (longitudinal, mediated by reduced negative affect). — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222302476_I_forgive_myself_now_I_can_study_How_self-forgiveness_for_procrastinating_can_reduce_future_procrastination)
- RCT (Mindfulness, 2025), "Effects and mechanisms of self-compassion on bedtime procrastination": 459 participants randomised to a 14-day online self-help self-compassion programme or waitlist; the intervention raised self-compassion, positive affect and future self-continuity and significantly reduced bedtime procrastination; positive affect and future self-continuity serially mediated the effect. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12671-025-02701-w)
- Guided internet-based procrastination interventions have been tested in RCTs with university students (details not retrieved). — [PMC12512977](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12512977/)
- Temptation bundling: Milkman, Minson & Volpp (2014) and Kirgios et al. (2020): see Key Question 5. — [PubMed 25843979](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25843979/); [OBHDP 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X)
- Fresh start: Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014, 2015); Beshears et al. (2021): see Key Question 5. — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf); [Beshears 2021 PDF](https://katherinemilkman.squarespace.com/s/using-fresh-starts-to-nudge-increased-retirement-savings.pdf)
- Two-minute rule: no empirical test located; origin is Allen's GTD. — [GTD site](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2020/05/the-two-minute-rule-2/)

### Inferences
- Because procrastination is driven by mood repair (Sirois), a reset screen that *punishes* missed targets will likely increase avoidance; a self-forgiveness line after a slip is the evidence-consistent design.
- Temptation bundling translates naturally to agency work: allow a favourite playlist/podcast only during a designated "grind" task; Kirgios et al. shows even teaching the idea has small durable effects.
- Fresh-start framing suggests making Monday and month-start resets visually distinct and explicitly closing the prior period.

#### Protocol (one screen, 60 seconds, shown after a missed target): "Reset after a slip"
1. "You didn't get to [task]. That happens to everyone; forgive yourself for it (one sentence)." (Wohl et al.)
2. "What's the 5-minute first step now?" (back into the if-then flow)
3. "Pair it with something you enjoy for the duration: [playlist / coffee / podcast]." (temptation bundling)

### Gaps
- No RCT of self-compassion or self-forgiveness on *work* procrastination (as opposed to bedtime or academic) was found.
- Temporal-discounting / present-bias meta-analyses (e.g., Steel 2007) were not retrieved; the brief's question on temporal discounting is unanswered here.
- The 2-minute rule remains untested.

## Key Question 13: What is overhyped or weak (frank list with status)

### Takeaway
The following popular practices have little, mixed, retracted or no evidence, or rest on misquoted figures. Where a practice is harmless and cheap it can stay as an *option*, but should not carry an "evidence-based" label in the product.

### Cited Findings
- **Multitasking "superpowers" / heavy media multitaskers are better**: the opposite direction is found; heavy media multitaskers show worse filtering and task switching (Ophir et al. 2009), the 2018 review finds "poorer performance in a number of cognitive domains", and replication meta-analyses find small/inconsistent effects, never an advantage. — [PubMed 19706386](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19706386/); [Stanford News on Uncapher & Wagner 2018](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2018/10/decade-data-reveals-heavy-multitaskers-reduced-memory-psychologist-says); [Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-017-1408-4)
- **10,000-hour rule**: Ericsson's 1993 data were about elite violinists' accumulated practice, not a universal threshold; Macnamara et al. 2014 meta-analysis: deliberate practice explains ~14% (95% CI 11-18%) of performance variance overall; Macnamara & Maitra 2019 attempted to replicate the violinist study. — [Macnamara et al. 2014 PDF](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/Macnamara-et-al.-2014.pdf); [PMC6731745](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6731745/)
- **Morning pages**: no direct studies; support is extrapolated from expressive writing. — [Morning pages guide acknowledging no trials](https://blog.mylifenote.ai/morning-pages/)
- **Eat the frog**: only indirect lab evidence (hard-first builds self-efficacy; Schroeder et al. 2020); no productivity trials. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103120303723)
- **Power hours / chronotype scheduling**: synchrony effect is not general or robust (>80% of studies show no chronotype main effect; Collabra 2023 null at latent level). — [Chronobiology International review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07420528.2025.2490495); [Collabra 2023](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/9/1/88337/197502/The-Interplay-of-Time-of-day-and-Chronotype)
- **Pomodoro as specified (25/5)**: never tested as such; Biwer et al. 2023 found no learning advantage for systematic breaks (24/6 or 12/3) over self-regulated ones, only mood/efficiency benefits. — [Wiley article](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjep.12593)
- **Time-management training**: Aeon, Faber & Panaccio (2021), PLoS ONE, meta-analysis of 158 studies, >53,000 respondents across four decades and six continents: time management is moderately related to job performance, academic achievement and well-being and negatively to distress; it improves well-being (especially life satisfaction) more than performance; conscientiousness is the only strong individual-difference correlate. — [PLoS ONE article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/citation?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0245066); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/02/210204131413.htm); a 2025 systematic review of workplace time-management interventions and well-being also exists. — [PubMed 42041057](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42041057/)
- **"Flow triggers" (Kotler: 17-22 triggers, "4% rule", "500% focus boost")**: these figures come from Kotler's own Flow Research Collective materials and popular articles, not peer-reviewed trials; Kotler himself labels some triggers as lacking data. — [Flow triggers list (practitioner site)](https://www.raizasali.com/post/flow-triggers-for-hacking-flow); [4% rule (Medium)](https://medium.com/@Vishen/the-4-rule-to-get-in-flow-84258ededec2); the peer-reviewed literature on flow onset is theoretical. — [Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763422004456)
- **Rituals reduce anxiety and improve performance (Brooks et al. 2016)**: retracted. — [Retraction notice](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597824000694)
- **Self-imposed costly deadlines (Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002)**: failed replication and retraction. — [Hyndman & Bisin 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976261460772); [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/)
- **"23 minutes to refocus" and "multitasking costs 40%"**: misquotes; the field study reports ~25 minutes with 2.3 intervening tasks and the 40% is a press paraphrase of sub-second lab switch costs. — [Tab Extension analysis](https://thetabextension.com/blog/cost-of-context-switching); [APA page](https://www.apa.org/topics/research/multitasking)
- **Phone in another room ("brain drain")**: preregistered replication null; meta-analytic d between -0.02 and -0.14. — [Ruiz Pardo & Minda 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691822002323); [Meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10525686/)
- **Zeigarnik "unfinished tasks are remembered better"**: no reliable memory advantage in the 2025 meta-analysis. — [Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w)
- **Write your worries before a high-stakes performance (Ramirez & Beilock 2011)**: did not replicate in the SSRP. — [Nature Human Behaviour 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0399-z)
- **Gratitude journaling as a productivity tool**: well-being effects small (d = 0.17 vs alternative activity; ~0 vs active controls), no productivity outcomes. — [Davis et al. 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/284163997_Thankful_for_the_Little_Things_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Gratitude_Interventions)

### Inferences
- Ranked by evidence for a 1-10 minute daily reset: (1) if-then plan for the first task; (2) recorded progress check with a specific daily target; (3) ready-to-resume note at switches; (4) shutdown: next steps for open loops + tomorrow's list + 2-minute lessons reflection; (5) scheduled breaks with preferred activities, earlier in the day; (6) notification batching / email at fixed times; (7) WOOP for a bigger goal weekly; (8) self-forgiveness line after slips; (9) temptation bundling and fresh-start framing as motivational wrappers; (10) sleep/light/hydration check-in as a gate on task choice. Everything in the "overhyped" list can be dropped or kept as unlabelled options.
- Several "strong" effects in this field are small in absolute terms (d 0.2-0.4) and came from students or health behaviours; the product should promise "fewer stalled starts and less end-of-day carry-over", not "X% more productive".

### Gaps
- Aeon & Aguinis (2017) narrative review was not retrieved directly; the 2021 meta-analysis by the same lead author is cited in its place.
- The "accountability partner 65%/95% (ASTD)" statistic could not be traced; treat as unsourced.
- Searches on "power hours" and on "flow triggers" peer-reviewed critiques were limited; no primary-source critique was pinned, only the absence of supporting trials.
- Any items where the primary page was blocked (noted throughout) carry numbers taken from search-engine summaries of those pages; a final editorial pass should spot-check the key figures (Harkin d = 0.40; Wang g = 0.336; Albulescu d = 0.36/0.35/0.16; Wittbrodt -0.21; Lim & Dinges -0.564; Macnamara 14%) against the PDFs once network access allows.
