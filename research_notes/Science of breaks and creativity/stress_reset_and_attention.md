# Stress reset and attention restoration in short work breaks (30 s to 15 min): what replicates

Scope note for the report writer: this session's web-search budget and every publisher fetch route (PMC, ScienceDirect, Wiley, Nature, PLOS, Frontiers, Springer, SAGE, Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar) were blocked or exhausted after the first search wave, so numbers below come from search-result excerpts of abstracts and secondary summaries rather than full-text reads. Every number carries its source; where a detail (sample size, CI, exact protocol) could not be confirmed it is listed under Gaps rather than guessed. Evidence grades used: **A** = meta-analysis of RCTs or multi-lab replication; **B** = at least one adequately powered RCT, not independently replicated; **C** = single small study / preliminary; **F** = failed replication or no evidence.

## Breathing: cyclic sighing, box breathing, resonance (~5.5-6 bpm) breathing, 4-7-8

### Takeaway
Slow breathing at roughly 6 breaths/min is the only breathing protocol with meta-analytic support for both an acute physiological effect (raised vagally-mediated HRV during and just after a single session) and a small-to-medium effect on self-reported stress across RCTs (g ~ -0.35); cyclic sighing rests on one Stanford RCT (n=108, 5 min/day for 28 days, no independent replication), box breathing has one new RCT (n=66) showing it buffers an acute stressor, and 4-7-8 has no acute office-relevant trial at all. Sessions under 5 minutes and fast-only breathing are the two features most associated with ineffective interventions in the systematic-review literature.

### Cited Findings
- Balban et al. 2023 (Cell Reports Medicine) randomised 108 participants to cyclic sighing, box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation or mindfulness meditation, practised 5 min/day for 28 days; cyclic sighing used a 1:2 inhale:exhale emphasis and produced reduced physiological arousal, less perceived stress and improved mood — [Physiopedia summary](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Cyclic_Sighing); [protocol.us summary](https://www.protocol.us/blog/cyclic-sighing-evidence)
- Cyclic sighing had the largest rise in positive affect over the month and was significantly better than mindfulness meditation, but it was NOT significantly better than the other two breathing conditions — [protocol.us summary](https://www.protocol.us/blog/cyclic-sighing-evidence)
- As of 2025 no independent lab has published a replication of Balban 2023; the study was led by Huberman (who popularised the technique) and Spiegel at Stanford; a registered trial (NCT07379606) comparing it with exercise exists — [Association for Applied Sport Psychology blog, 2025](https://appliedsportpsych.org/blog/2025/06/the-commodification-of-breath-parsing-fact-from-fiction-in-the-gilded-age-of-breathwork-and-breathing-influencers/)
- A 2025/2026 study titled "Dissecting Cardiovascular Responses to a Fixed-Interval Volitional Sighing Protocol Using a Mixed Modeling Approach" exists in PMC (content not retrievable this session) — [PMC12811738](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12811738/)
- A 2026 pilot just-in-time ecological momentary intervention study compared ONE-MINUTE cyclic sighing vs box breathing for acute anxiety reduction and attention in real life (results not retrievable this session) — [Anxiety, Stress & Coping 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10615806.2026.2659809)
- Fincham et al. 2023 meta-analysis of breathwork RCTs: subjective stress, 12 RCTs, N=785, g = -0.35 [95% CI -0.55, -0.14], p=0.0009; anxiety k=20, g=-0.32; depressive symptoms k=18, g=-0.40 — [Scientific Reports 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-27247-y); [PMC9828383](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9828383/)
- Laborde et al. 2022 meta-analysis (Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews): voluntary slow breathing increased vagally-mediated HRV during practice (RMSSD g=0.53, medium; LF power g=1.49, large) and a small RMSSD increase persisted immediately after a single session; effects also found after multi-session interventions — [Solent University record](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/effects-of-voluntary-slow-breathing-on-heart-rate-and-heart-rate-/); [secondary summary, The Breathing Diabetic](https://www.thebreathingdiabetic.com/science-411s/laborde-et-al-2022)
- Bentley et al. 2023 systematic review (Brain Sciences): 58 studies, 72 interventions, 54 effective; effective practices avoided sessions shorter than 5 minutes and fast-only breath paces, and included human-guided training, multiple sessions and long-term practice; six breaths per minute significantly reduced anxiety and heart rate; standing for long periods, interruptions and inadequate training for technical practices rendered interventions ineffective — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Breathing-Practices-for-Stress-and-Anxiety-of-Based-Bentley-D%E2%80%99Andrea-Penna/b9ed440871b48c903451b4807b4a5c475cd643cc); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375813907_Breathing_Practices_for_Stress_and_Anxiety_Reduction_Conceptual_Framework_of_Implementation_Guidelines_Based_on_a_Systematic_Review_of_the_Published_Literature)
- Goessl, Curtiss & Hofmann 2017 meta-analysis of HRV biofeedback for stress and anxiety: 24 studies, N=484; within-group pre-post g=0.81; between-group vs control g=0.83; efficacy was NOT moderated by number of sessions, study year, risk of bias, sex ratio or anxiety-disorder status — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316802722_The_effect_of_heart_rate_variability_biofeedback_training_on_stress_and_anxiety_a_meta-analysis); [PDF](https://www.heartmath-europe.com/doc/Goessl%20Hofmann%202017%20HRV_BF%20meta_stress_anxiety.pdf)
- Lehrer et al. 2020 meta-analysis (Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback 45(3):109-129): HRV biofeedback / paced breathing at ~6 breaths/min gave medium effects on anxiety and depressive symptoms and a small effect on stress; the baroreflex produces resonant effects between 4.5 and 6.5 breaths/min — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341254440_Heart_Rate_Variability_Biofeedback_Improves_Emotional_and_Physical_Health_and_Performance_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta_Analysis); [Pizzoli et al. 2021 summary of Lehrer](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7988005/)
- Resonance frequency is not always stable over time within a person and relates to inter-beat interval, and a 2026 four-week RCT compared individually assessed resonance frequency vs fixed 0.1 Hz (6 bpm) breathing — [Scientific Reports 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-87867-8); [Scientific Reports 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-53333-6)
- Box breathing vs prolonged exhalation (Röttger et al. 2021, Stroop under time pressure and noise): prolonged exhalation was superior on task performance, while physiological arousal was lower under box breathing — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343451194_The_Effectiveness_of_Combat_Tactical_Breathing_as_Compared_with_Prolonged_Exhalation)
- New RCT (n=66; 22 per arm: normal breathing, prolonged exhalation, box breathing) performed before a virtual Trier Social Stress Test: both prolonged exhalation and box breathing attenuated post-stressor rises in heart rate, state anxiety and salivary alpha-amylase vs normal breathing — [PMC13321280](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13321280/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S266649762600024X)
- A 2026 systematic review of RCTs on box breathing for stress, autonomic and clinical outcomes exists (Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback; content not retrievable) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10484-026-09812-7)
- Tactical (box) breathing improved police performance in a critical-incident simulation (Collabra 2025) and marksmanship in student officers (PMC 2024) — [Collabra](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/11/1/144527/213538/Tactical-Breathing-Enhances-Police-Performance-in); [PMC11622642](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11622642/)
- 4-7-8 breathing (inhale nose 4 s, hold 7 s, exhale mouth 8 s) has only been tested in multi-week clinical trials: tinnitus patients (23 vs 25, 6 weeks), nursing students' sleep (4 weeks), and a 64-participant trial on anxiety/sleep during treatment; no acute single-session trial in healthy workers was found — [PMC12895279](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12895279/); [NCT06912750](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06912750)
- Fincham et al. also ran a randomised PLACEBO-controlled trial of coherent breathing (Scientific Reports 2023); results not retrievable this session — [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49279-8)

### Inferences
- The protocol with the most convergent evidence for a guided timer is slow breathing at ~6 breaths/min (10-second cycle; e.g., 4 s in / 6 s out or 5 s in / 5 s out, nasal, seated, uninterrupted) for a minimum of 5 minutes. The HRV effect is reliable during the session (Laborde 2022), the stress effect is small-to-medium (Fincham 2023), and the review-level guidance is explicit that <5 min sessions tend to be ineffective (Bentley 2023).
- Cyclic sighing should be shipped as "preliminary, grade B": one RCT, cumulative effects over 28 days, no independent replication, and in its own trial it did not beat box breathing or cyclic hyperventilation. If offered, use the Balban-style dose: 5 minutes, repeated double inhale through the nose followed by a long mouth exhale with a ~1:2 inhale:exhale emphasis (the exact per-breath timings were not retrievable; see Gaps).
- Box breathing (4-4-4-4) is best framed as a pre-stressor buffer (before a pitch or client call) rather than a general reset, on the strength of the n=66 TSST RCT and the police studies; it is grade B.
- 4-7-8 should not be presented as evidence-based for acute stress in an office; it is a sleep/clinical protocol with no acute trial.
- Breathing works without equipment and sitting at a desk; HRV biofeedback adds a sensor but Goessl 2017 found the number of sessions did not moderate efficacy, which argues that a paced-breathing timer without biofeedback captures most of the practical value.

### Gaps
- Exact per-breath timings and instructions from Balban 2023 (inhale durations, number of breaths per minute, whether eyes closed) could not be read from the primary paper this session; the 1:2 ratio and 5-min dose come from secondary summaries.
- Effect sizes (Cohen's d) for the Balban positive-affect and respiratory-rate changes were not retrievable.
- The results of the 2026 one-minute cyclic sighing vs box breathing EMA study, the 2025/26 volitional-sighing cardiovascular study, and Fincham's placebo-controlled coherent-breathing RCT could not be read; these three are the most important documents for deciding whether a 1-minute protocol is defensible and whether 6 bpm beats a sham pace.
- Publication-bias and risk-of-bias assessments inside Fincham 2023 and Laborde 2022 were not retrievable.
- Total study count in Laborde 2022 is reported as 172 by a secondary summary; unverified.

## Micro-breaks (<10 min): Albulescu et al. 2022 and related trials

### Takeaway
Micro-breaks reliably but modestly raise vigor and reduce fatigue (d ~ 0.35), and their effect on task performance is small and non-significant overall, appearing only for less cognitively demanding tasks and growing with break length; for creative/cognitive work the honest claim is "micro-breaks restore how you feel, not measurably how you perform".

### Cited Findings
- Albulescu et al. 2022 (PLOS ONE) systematic review and meta-analysis: 19 records, 22 independent samples, N=2,335; micro-breaks defined as breaks of 10 minutes or less — [PMC9432722](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Effects: vigor d=0.36 (p<.001; k=9, n=913); fatigue d=0.35 (p<.001; k=9, n=803); overall performance d=0.16 (p=.116, non-significant; k=15, n=1,132) — [PMC9432722](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Performance subgroup analysis: significant effects only for tasks with lower cognitive demands; meta-regression: the longer the break, the greater the performance boost — [PLOS ONE PDF](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0272460&type=printable)
- Bergouignan et al. 2016 (Int J Behav Nutr Phys Act), randomised crossover, 30 sedentary adults, three 6-hour conditions: uninterrupted sitting; one 30-min morning moderate treadmill walk; six hourly 5-min moderate treadmill "microbouts". Both activity conditions raised self-perceived energy and POMS vigor vs sitting; only the hourly 5-min microbouts also improved mood, lowered end-of-day fatigue and reduced food cravings, with no cost to cognitive performance — [PMC5094084](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5094084/); [HAL PDF](https://hal.science/hal-01392105v1/file/Bergouignan_2016_IntJBehavNutrPhysAct.pdf)
- Biwer et al. 2023 (British Journal of Educational Psychology) compared self-regulated breaks (n=35), Pomodoro-style 24 min work / 6 min break (n=25) and systematic-short 12 min / 3 min (n=27): no group differences in invested mental effort or task completion; systematic breaks had mood benefits and apparent efficiency benefits (similar completion in less time); self-regulated students chose longer study sessions, associated with higher fatigue and lower concentration and motivation — [Wiley abstract](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/bjep.12593); [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel/)
- Follow-up studies exist comparing self-regulated, Pomodoro and Flowtime break-taking (2025) and systematic short vs self-regulated breaks on subjective experience and actual learning (2026); contents not retrievable — [PMC12292963](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12292963/); [Behavioral Sciences 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16071158)

### Inferences
- For a team OS, set expectations honestly: a ≤10-min break buys roughly a third of a standard deviation in vigor/fatigue; do not promise output gains for creative work.
- Because the meta-regression shows longer breaks give larger performance gains and cognitively demanding tasks showed no significant performance effect, a 5-10 minute break is a better default than 30-60 s for anything beyond clerical work.
- The strongest single-trial design evidence for office workers (Bergouignan) favours many short movement breaks (5 min hourly) over one longer bout for mood and fatigue.
- Imposed, scheduled breaks (Pomodoro-like) appear at least as good as self-chosen breaks and prevent people from over-running; this supports a default timer rather than "take breaks when you feel like it".

### Gaps
- Albulescu's moderator results by break ACTIVITY (relaxation vs social vs cognitive vs physical) could not be read from the full text this session; only the task-demand and duration moderators were confirmed.
- Publication-bias tests in Albulescu 2022 not retrievable.
- Steidle et al. 2017 ("energizing respites") and other activity-type comparisons (e.g., Blasche et al. 2018 boxing vs relaxation vs chatting) could not be searched before the budget ran out.

## Nature exposure: 40-s green roof, Attention Restoration Theory, nature sounds and video

### Takeaway
Nature exposure has small, heterogeneous effects on attention (meta-analytic effect sizes roughly 0.2-0.3, most consistent at 30 minutes or more); the famous 40-second green-roof result is a single unreplicated study, and a well-powered direct test of pictures/sounds (n=202) found no restoration at all, so very brief nature micro-breaks should be offered for affect and relaxation, not promised as attention restorers.

### Cited Findings
- Lee et al. 2015 (Journal of Environmental Psychology): 150 university students did a sustained-attention task (SART), were randomised to a 40-second view of a flowering-meadow green roof or a bare concrete roof, then repeated the task; the green-roof group showed sustained attention and fewer errors than the concrete group, interpreted via Attention Restoration Theory — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/40-second-green-roof-views-sustain-attention:-The-Lee-Williams/662dbfb1a298df2c35a379bb5c07ac437e666753); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/05/150526093611.htm)
- Ohly et al. 2016 meta-analysis of 31 ART studies with objective cognitive outcomes: nature improved Digit Span Forward/Backward and Trail Making Test B, but NON-natural control settings improved the Search and Memory Task, i.e., effects are test-specific — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022 summarising Ohly](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.809629/full)
- 2025 meta-analysis (Journal of Environmental Psychology, vol. 104): 80 studies, 273 outcomes; effect sizes small (~0.20-0.30) with substantial heterogeneity; only Working Memory and Attentional Control showed reliable nature benefits; the largest natural-vs-non-natural difference appears at about 30 minutes of exposure and benefits were more consistent for exposures of 30 min or longer — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S027249442500115X); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391940254_The_Relationship_Between_Nature_Exposures_and_Attention_Restoration_as_Moderated_by_Exposure_Duration_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-analysis)
- Emfield & Neider 2014 (Frontiers in Psychology): 202 participants did reverse digit span, the Attention Network Task and a functional-field-of-view task before and after a restoration period in one of seven conditions (natural or urban sounds, images, both, or neither); no differential restoration effect in any task, although nature images and sounds were rated more relaxing — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00548/full)
- Other published nulls: "Restoration of sustained attention following virtual nature exposure: Undeniable or unreliable?" and "Sometimes Nature Doesn't Work: Absence of Attention Restoration in Older Adults Exposed to Environmental Scenes" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343681114_Restoration_of_sustained_attention_following_virtual_nature_exposure_Undeniable_or_unreliable); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333659127_Sometimes_Nature_Doesn't_Work_Absence_of_Attention_Restoration_in_Older_Adults_Exposed_to_Environmental_Scenes)
- Van Hedger et al. 2019: listening to natural soundscapes improved backward digit span and dual n-back performance more than urban sounds — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022 summarising Van Hedger](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.809629/full); contradicted on sounds by [Emfield & Neider 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00548/full)
- A 2024 meta-analysis on natural sound exposure and health recovery exists (Science of the Total Environment; not retrievable) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048969724011914)
- A vigilance study titled "Rest improves performance, nature improves happiness: Assessment of break periods on the abbreviated vigilance task" (Consciousness and Cognition 2016) exists; the title indicates nature-content breaks improved affect while plain rest improved performance (full results not retrievable) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1053810016300526)

### Inferences
- A "look at something green for 40 s" micro-break is grade C (one study, n=150, no replication located); the bigger literature says very brief virtual nature does not reliably restore attention, so it should be positioned as a pleasant, zero-cost option that may lift mood.
- For a real attention effect the evidence points to ~30 min outdoors, which is a lunch-walk recommendation rather than a break-timer feature.
- Window views or a real green roof (Lee 2015 used a real view) may differ from screen images, which is what most null studies used; that is a plausible but untested reason for the discrepancy.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes and exact statistics for Lee 2015 not retrievable; no direct replication of the 40-s protocol was found in searches.
- Berman et al. 2008 (nature walk and backward digit span) and Stevenson et al. 2018 meta-analysis could not be fetched; their effect sizes are not reported here.
- Van Hedger 2019 sample size and effect sizes not retrievable.

## Naps and rest: 10-min nap, nap vs caffeine, office feasibility

### Takeaway
A 10-minute nap is the best-supported short-nap dose: it produced immediate gains in alertness, fatigue, vigor and cognitive performance lasting up to ~155 min, whereas 5 min did little and 20-30 min naps caused sleep inertia; the evidence base is small lab studies, and the office constraint (a quiet, dark place and the ability to fall asleep within minutes) is the real limiting factor.

### Cited Findings
- Brooks & Lack 2006 (Sleep): after nocturnal sleep restriction, naps of 5, 10, 20 and 30 min were compared with no nap; the 10-min nap produced immediate improvements in all outcomes (sleep latency, subjective sleepiness, fatigue, vigor, cognitive performance), some maintained for up to 155 min; the 5-min nap produced few benefits; the 30-min nap caused a period of impaired alertness and performance immediately after waking (sleep inertia) with improvements only 35-95 min later — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16796222/); [Sleep abstract](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article-abstract/29/6/831/2708239)
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of afternoon napping and cognition (Sleep Medicine Reviews 2022) exists; effect sizes not retrievable — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S108707922200079X)
- Mednick et al. 2008 (Behavioural Brain Research): 61 participants; a 60-90 min afternoon nap vs 200 mg caffeine vs placebo; the nap improved verbal recall at 7 h 20 min relative to both; caffeine impaired motor learning relative to placebo and nap; nap and caffeine were equivalent for perceptual learning; placebo beat caffeine on all three tasks — [NIMH summary](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/news/science-updates/2008/caffeine-no-substitute-for-a-nap-to-enhance-memory); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5301521_Comparing_the_benefits_of_Caffeine_Naps_and_Placebo_on_Verbal_Motor_and_Perceptual_Memory)
- 2025 study on optimally timed automatic awakening from a short daytime nap (cognitive performance, alertness, fatigue) exists — [Scientific Reports 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-21008-3)

### Inferences
- If a nap option is included, the timer should be 10 minutes of sleep opportunity with a hard wake-up and a note that 20-30 min naps may leave the person groggy for 30+ min; this requires a quiet, dim room or nap pod, which most offices cannot provide, so treat it as conditional.
- The nap-vs-caffeine comparisons that favour naps used long naps (60-90 min) and memory outcomes, not 10-min office naps and attention; do not generalise them.

### Gaps
- Brooks & Lack 2006 sample size and effect sizes not retrievable (it is a small within-subjects lab study).
- Lovato & Lack and the 2022 Leong et al. meta-analysis effect sizes and duration moderators not retrievable.

## Movement: walking, stair climbing, stretching; acute exercise and executive function

### Takeaway
Ten minutes of easy stair walking raised feelings of energy more than 50 mg caffeine in one small study, and hourly 5-min walks reliably raised vigor in a crossover trial, but meta-analytic evidence says cognitive (executive-function) benefits need at least ~11-20 min of moderate exercise; so movement micro-breaks are an energy/mood tool, with cognition gains requiring a longer bout.

### Cited Findings
- Randolph & O'Connor 2017 (Physiology & Behavior): 10 min of low-to-moderate intensity stair walking vs a 50 mg caffeine capsule vs placebo in physically active young women who were habitual caffeine users with chronic insufficient sleep; stair walking increased POMS vigor post-exercise vs both caffeine and placebo; the effect faded and groups were similar one hour later — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031938416310666); [UGA news](https://news.uga.edu/stairs-more-energy-research/)
- A 2022 follow-up asked whether 4 min of stair walking is enough for energy/fatigue benefits (Frontiers in Psychology; results not retrievable) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.895446/full)
- Bergouignan 2016: hourly 5-min moderate walks increased energy and vigor and reduced fatigue vs 6 h sitting (details in micro-break section) — [PMC5094084](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5094084/)
- Chang et al. 2012 meta-analysis (Brain Research) on acute exercise and cognition: a minimum of 11 min of exercise was required to produce cognitive changes, with no improvement for bouts of 10 min or less — [secondary report in PMC8992970](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8992970/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223962303_The_effects_of_acute_exercise_on_cognitive_performance_A_meta-analysis)
- Meta-analytic results consistently support a positive effect of acute exercise on executive functions, especially in older adults — [PMC8992970](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8992970/)
- Further meta-analyses: Oberste et al. 2019 on acute aerobic exercise and interference control; Moreau & Chou 2019 on high-intensity exercise and executive function; a 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis on acute physical activity and cognition in young adults; an individual-participant-data meta-analysis on acute aerobic exercise and executive function — [Frontiers 2019 PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.528352/pdf); [Moreau & Chou](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691619850568); [Nature Comms Psychology 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-024-00124-2); [ScienceDirect IPD](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763421002670)

### Inferences
- Minimal effective dose for FEELING energised: ~5-10 min of walking or stair climbing (Bergouignan; Randolph & O'Connor), repeated hourly if possible; effect on vigor lasts well under an hour.
- Minimal effective dose for measurable executive-function gains: ≥11 min, more typically 20 min of moderate aerobic activity (Chang 2012); this is a lunchtime walk, not a micro-break.
- Stretching: no evidence located in this session (see Gaps); do not claim a cognitive benefit.
- Stair climbing needs stairs and clothes you can move in; walking works in any office corridor.

### Gaps
- Chang 2012 overall effect size and the delayed-testing moderator values not retrievable (the full-text report gives several small effect sizes by timing; unverified here).
- Randolph & O'Connor 2017 sample size and effect size not retrievable (small, single-sex sample).
- No studies on stretching breaks and attention were found before the search budget ran out.

## Eye and posture breaks: 20-20-20 and standing

### Takeaway
The 20-20-20 rule is advice, not an evidence-based prescription: one lab trial found changing the break interval did not change reading speed, accuracy or symptoms, systematic reviews rate the evidence low-certainty, and the single supportive two-week trial could not be verified in detail.

### Cited Findings
- Johnson & Rosenfield 2023 (Optometry and Vision Science, "20-20-20 Rule: Are These Numbers Justified?"): 30 participants, four sessions 24 h apart with different break intervals; varying the interval between breaks did not significantly change reading speed, reading accuracy or reported symptoms; authors concluded the results do not support 20-second scheduled breaks as a therapeutic intervention for digital eye strain — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36473088/); [Eyes On Eyecare summary](https://glance.eyesoneyecare.com/stories/2023-01-25/20-20-20-rule-may-not-actually-help-digital-eye-strain/)
- Talens-Estarelles et al. 2023 (Contact Lens and Anterior Eye) tested the 20-20-20 rule on digital eye strain, dry eye and binocular vision over two weeks; a secondary summary claims a 37% reduction in eye-strain scores with strict adherence — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1367048422001990); claim reported by [FavTray guide (non-peer-reviewed)](https://favtray.com/blog/20-20-20-rule-complete-guide)
- A 2023 systematic review of digital eye strain in young screen users found that structured advice to follow the 20/20/20 rule did not seem to alleviate symptoms — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0091743523000737)

### Inferences
- Keep 20-20-20 as an optional, harmless prompt (it is also a de facto micro-break) but label it "comfort, low-certainty evidence", not "science-backed".
- Nothing retrieved supports standing breaks for attention or stress; any claim there is unsupported in these notes.

### Gaps
- The Talens-Estarelles trial's sample size, results and whether benefits persisted could not be verified; the 37% figure comes from a blog.
- The search summary mentioned a 2023 Cochrane review rating break-based strategies as low-certainty evidence; no direct Cochrane link was obtained, so treat as unverified.
- No sit-stand desk or standing-break cognition/stress evidence was gathered.

## Relaxation techniques: brief mindfulness, PMR, body scan; honest effect sizes

### Takeaway
Brief mindfulness training reduces negative affect by a small amount (g=0.21 across 65 RCTs) that shrinks to about zero (g=0.04) after publication-bias correction, longer programmes reach ~0.3-0.4 for anxiety in clinical meta-analysis, and single-session PMR shows acute physiological/VAS relief but not state-anxiety change; this is a "modest, honest" category, not a headline feature.

### Cited Findings
- Schumer, Lindsay & Creswell 2018 (Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology): 65 RCTs, N=5,489; brief mindfulness training reduced negative affectivity vs controls, g=0.21 (p<.001); after accounting for publication bias the effect fell to g=0.04; community samples showed larger effects than student samples — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Brief-Mindfulness-Training-for-Negative-A-Review-Schumer-Lindsay/18e7d1b93185a53d7f37a4a39c75e3c4c0f690fc)
- Goyal et al. 2014 (JAMA Internal Medicine): 47 trials, N=3,320; mindfulness meditation programmes had moderate evidence of improved anxiety, effect size 0.38 (95% CI 0.12-0.64) at 8 weeks and 0.22 (0.02-0.43) at 3-6 months — [JAMA Internal Medicine](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1809754); [AHRQ report](https://effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/meditation_research.pdf)
- Van Dam et al. 2018 "Mind the Hype" (Perspectives on Psychological Science): critiques poor methodology, definitional problems and incentive conflicts in mindfulness research, aiming to curb misinformation about benefits — [Mount Sinai record](https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/mind-the-hype-a-critical-evaluation-and-prescriptive-agenda-for-r-2/); reply exchange with Davidson & Dahl — [Perspectives on Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691617727529)
- A randomised clinical trial of a SINGLE-session mindfulness-based intervention was published in PLOS ONE 2024 (results not retrievable) — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0299300)
- A 3-minute mindfulness intervention study on critical-incident decision-making exists (results not retrievable) — [PMC8194826](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8194826/)
- Progressive muscle relaxation: a single pre-ECT PMR session significantly reduced VAS anxiety and physiological indicators but produced no significant change in State-Trait Anxiety Inventory scores — [PMC12482224](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12482224/)
- A meta-analysis reported PMR reduced anxiety with SMD -1.11 (95% CI -1.69 to -0.53; I²=85%), effective across intervention durations (population appears clinical; see Gaps) — [PMC12195441](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12195441/)
- A 2024 systematic review found PMR effective for stress, anxiety and depression in adults, with effects varying by population and setting — [Psychology Research and Behavior Management](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2147/PRBM.S437277)
- A 2026 meta-analysis of RCTs reports PMR improves sleep quality and mental health — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41633054/)

### Inferences
- A 3-10 minute guided mindfulness audio is defensible as a low-cost option, but the best-estimate acute effect on mood is small and may be near zero once publication bias is considered; do not market it as a stress "reset".
- Short PMR (tense 5 s / release 10-15 s through major muscle groups for ~5 min) plausibly lowers bodily tension markers in a single session; evidence for state anxiety change in one session is negative, so position it as a physical-tension tool.
- The very large PMR SMD (-1.11) with 85% heterogeneity in a likely clinical population should not be quoted for healthy knowledge workers.

### Gaps
- Basso et al. 2019 (13-min daily meditation for 8 weeks) and Howarth et al. 2019 (review of brief mindfulness interventions) could not be searched before the budget ran out; no numbers are reported for them.
- Which meta-analysis the PMR SMD -1.11 belongs to, and its population, could not be confirmed.
- No body-scan-specific acute trial was located.

## Cold exposure and the dive reflex (cold water on face/wrists)

### Takeaway
Cold facial immersion with breath-hold reliably triggers the diving response (heart rate falls, vagal activity rises), and one small crossover study found it cut panic symptoms in panic-disorder patients, but there is no meta-analytic evidence that it reduces everyday work stress, and cold applied while breathing normally tends to provoke a cold-shock arousal response instead.

### Cited Findings
- Facial immersion in cold water reduces heart rate and cardiac output while increasing total peripheral resistance and mean arterial, systolic and diastolic pressure — [Advances in Physiology Education](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/advan.00125.2013)
- Ackermann et al. 2023 (Psychophysiology) published a systematic review and meta-analysis of the diving response and cardiac vagal activity (effect sizes not retrievable) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/psyp.14183)
- In healthy participants and panic-disorder patients exposed in random order to cold facial immersion, breath holding and CO2 challenge, the clinical group showed significant reductions in both physiological and cognitive panic symptoms after cold facial immersion — [PMC8667218](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8667218/)
- A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of cold-water immersion (11 RCTs, N=3,177 healthy adults) on health and wellbeing exists; outcome details not retrievable — [PMC11778651](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11778651/)
- A non-peer-reviewed clinical website argues that an ice pack or cold water applied while breathing normally produces a cold-shock response (spikes in heart rate, breathing, blood pressure and adrenaline) rather than the diving reflex, which can worsen symptoms in anxious people — [AnxietyCentre (opinion, not peer-reviewed)](https://www.anxietycentre.com/articles/why-the-cold-diving-response-is-not-appropriate-for-anxiety-disorder-recovery/)

### Inferences
- Protocol-level guidance that is physiologically grounded: hold the breath and immerse the face (forehead/eyes/cheeks) in cold water (~10-15 °C) for 15-30 s to trigger bradycardia; wrists alone are not the trigeminal trigger and have no supporting evidence located here. Grade: physiology A, stress-outcome C.
- It needs a sink and cold water and is socially awkward at a desk; the panic-symptom finding is in a clinical population and should not be generalised to ordinary work stress.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes from Ackermann 2023 and the 2025 CWI meta-analysis (including whether perceived stress changes immediately vs hours later) not retrievable.
- Jungmann et al. 2018 (cold stimulation of the neck and vagal activation) could not be searched.
- No RCT of cold face/wrist water for acute stress in healthy workers was located.

## Music during breaks

### Takeaway
Music reliably reduces stress-related outcomes in meta-analyses (music therapy d ≈ 0.72; music listening in healthy people has its own 2022 meta-analysis), but no decent evidence was found that a music break restores attention or vigilance, so music belongs in the "mood/stress" column, not the "attention" column.

### Cited Findings
- de Witte et al. 2020 (Health Psychology Review): meta-analytic review of 47 studies showed medium-to-strong effects of music therapy (d=0.723) on psychological and physiological stress-related outcomes — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2020.1846580); [UvA PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/111312923/Music_therapy_for_stress_reduction_a_systematic_review_and_meta_analysis.pdf)
- de Witte et al. 2020 also published a systematic review with two meta-analyses of music INTERVENTIONS on stress-related outcomes (effect sizes not retrievable this session) — [UvA PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/58927620/Effects_of_music_interventions_on_stress_related_outcomes_a_systematic_review_and_two_meta_analyses.pdf)
- A 2022 PLOS ONE systematic review with meta-analysis of experimental studies on music listening and stress recovery in healthy individuals exists (results not retrievable) — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0270031)
- A 2024 Leisure Sciences study compared natural sounds with favourite music as repeated indoor restorative activities (results not retrievable) — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01490400.2024.2446802)
- Searches for music breaks and attention restoration in knowledge workers returned no primary studies on attention outcomes — [search summary for de Witte queries](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2020.1846580)

### Inferences
- A short self-selected music break is a reasonable, zero-equipment stress tool; the d ≈ 0.72 figure is for therapist-delivered music therapy, so quote it with that caveat and expect smaller effects from passive listening.
- Given the vigilance literature's "rest is best" finding (below), a music break should be a non-task activity rather than background music while working.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for music listening (as opposed to music therapy) and the 2022 Adiasto-type meta-analysis are not reported because the papers were unreachable.
- No study was found testing music breaks on attention or vigilance outcomes.

## Timing: vigilance decrement, brief diversions, ultradian 90-min claim, Pomodoro, DeskTime 52/17

### Takeaway
The vigilance literature supports imposing rest breaks (true rest beats switching to another task), the specific "brief diversion" mechanism of Ariga & Lleras 2011 has not replicated consistently, the 90-minute waking ultradian cycle is unproven, Pomodoro-style fixed schedules are supported by one modest trial on mood/efficiency, and DeskTime's 52/17 is observational app data, not science.

### Cited Findings
- Ariga & Lleras 2011 (Cognition, "Brief and rare mental breaks keep you focused"): proposed that deactivating and reactivating task goals via brief diversions preempts vigilance decrements and advised imposing brief breaks during long tasks — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222820913_Brief_and_rare_mental_breaks_keep_you_focused_Deactivation_and_reactivation_of_task_goals_preempt_vigilance_decrements); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/02/110208131529.htm)
- Follow-up studies have not found consistent support for the Ariga & Lleras effect — [University of Canterbury thesis "The effect of disruptions on vigilance"](https://ir.canterbury.ac.nz/bitstreams/d37ea036-2be8-4f52-b65a-a432e60d87a1/download); [Ross, Russell & Helton, "Effects of breaks and goal switches on the vigilance decrement"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260382553_Effects_of_breaks_and_goal_switches_on_the_vigilance_decrement)
- Helton & Russell 2015 (Cognition, "Rest is best"): post-interruption vigilance was best after a rest break, worst in the continuous condition; switching to a verbal-memory, spatial-memory or alphanumeric vigilance task during the break beat continuous work but did not restore performance as well as passive rest; authors infer the decrement reflects repeated use of specific executive resources plus domain-specific interference — [PDF](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Rest-is-best:-The-role-of-rest-and-task-on-Helton-Russell/18cd64e95b8e35bc2e4c3159b1009fb4b02df407)
- A follow-up ("Disrupting monotony while increasing demand: benefits of rest and intervening tasks on vigilance", Psychological Research) and a 2023 paper "Rest breaks aid directed attention and learning" extend this — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-016-0752-7); [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20590776.2023.2225700)
- Recent computational modelling found only minimal vigilance changes in a cognitive monitoring task, and a 2025/26 analysis attributes the decrement to changes in bias, lapse rate and guess rate rather than sensitivity — [Computational Brain & Behavior](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42113-024-00233-5); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42265515/)
- Kleitman's Basic Rest-Activity Cycle is a hypothesised ~90-min (80-120 min) rhythm observed in sleep and proposed to continue in waking; evidence for the waking cycle is less settled, the period varies between people and days, and a 1995 study by Monk et al. looked for a 1.5-h rhythm in cognitive performance and did not find one — [Wikipedia: BRAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_rest%E2%80%93activity_cycle); [secondary blog summary citing Monk 1995 (non-peer-reviewed)](https://www.temporacy.com/the-90-minute/)
- Biwer et al. 2023: fixed Pomodoro-style breaks (24/6 or 12/3) gave mood and efficiency benefits over self-regulated breaks, with no difference in effort or task completion — [Wiley](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/bjep.12593)
- DeskTime's 52/17 "rule" came from a 2014 analysis of its own time-tracking app data on the top 10% most productive users (52 min work, 17 min away from tracked work); the data are observational and cannot show that the breaks caused productivity; a 2021 re-analysis found 112 min work / 26 min break — [DeskTime blog](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17-updated/); [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/t-desktime-52-17-work-break-productivity-observational-data/)

### Inferences
- Design implication: a break should be genuine disengagement (eyes off screen, no email, no Slack) because task-switch "breaks" restore less than passive rest (Helton & Russell 2015).
- There is no empirical basis for hard-coding 90-minute focus blocks; a fixed 25-50 min cycle with a scheduled break is at least as defensible and has one trial behind it (Biwer 2023).
- How long until performance declines: the vigilance literature shows decrements within the first 20-30 min of monotonous monitoring tasks, but no retrievable study maps this onto creative/knowledge work; treat any "attention drops after X minutes" number as unsupported.

### Gaps
- Ariga & Lleras 2011 sample size, task length and diversion frequency could not be read from the primary PDF (blocked).
- Helton & Russell 2015 sample size, task length and break length not retrievable.
- Monk et al. 1995 citation is from a blog; the primary paper was not located.
- Results of the 2025 and 2026 Pomodoro/Flowtime studies not retrievable.

## What is overhyped or fails to replicate: power posing, ego depletion, binaural beats, ASMR, brain breaks, 20-20-20, cyclic sighing hype

### Takeaway
Power posing's hormonal/behavioural claims and ego depletion both failed large preregistered replications (ego depletion d ≈ 0.04), binaural beats show a medium meta-analytic effect undermined by inconclusive evidence of any brain entrainment, ASMR produces a measurable but tiny heart-rate drop only in responders, and the 20-20-20 rule and unreplicated cyclic-sighing claims are routinely overstated.

### Cited Findings
- Hagger et al. 2016 multilab preregistered replication of ego depletion reported d=0.04 with a Bayes factor favouring the null by roughly 4:1; Vohs et al. 2021 ran a second multisite preregistered test using a paradigm Baumeister endorsed as canonical, and large-scale replications found the effect close to zero — [Perspectives on Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616652873); [ScienceDirect 2025 commentary](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691825006596)
- Ranehill et al. 2015 (n=200) registered replication failed to reproduce power posing's hormonal effects, and first author Dana Carney disavowed the original finding in 2016 — [Replication Crisis Hub (secondary)](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/)
- Garcia-Argibay et al. 2019 (Psychological Research) meta-analysis of binaural beats: 35 effect sizes, overall g=0.45 on cognition, anxiety and pain, with effects depending on frequency, exposure time and timing — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-018-1066-8)
- Ingendoh et al. 2023 (PLOS ONE) systematic review: the literature on brainwave entrainment from binaural beats is "inconclusive at best" — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0286023); [PMC10198548](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10198548/)
- A 2022 meta-analysis on binaural beats for memory and attention exists (Psychological Research) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-022-01706-7)
- Poerio et al. 2018: people who experience ASMR showed a significantly greater heart-rate reduction when watching ASMR videos (average 3.14 bpm) than non-experiencers, alongside increased skin conductance — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/06/180621101334.htm); a 2025 study asked whether ASMR content is "more relaxing than nature" on parasympathetic measures — [Neuroscience of Consciousness](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2025/1/niaf012/8127084)
- Breathing-influencer claims about cyclic sighing outrun the single unreplicated trial — [AASP blog 2025](https://appliedsportpsych.org/blog/2025/06/the-commodification-of-breath-parsing-fact-from-fiction-in-the-gilded-age-of-breathwork-and-breathing-influencers/)
- 20-20-20: see the eye-break section; lab trial found no symptom effect of interval variation — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36473088/)

### Inferences
- Do not include power-pose prompts, "willpower is a depletable tank" framing, binaural-beat audio marketed as focus-enhancing, or ASMR as a general stress reset; the first two are refuted, the latter two are small/population-specific effects with weak mechanistic support.
- The Garcia-Argibay g=0.45 should be read against Ingendoh 2023: if there is no reliable entrainment, any benefit is likely relaxation/expectancy, which ordinary calm music also provides.

### Gaps
- Primary links for Ranehill 2015, Cuddy et al. 2018 p-curve reply, Gronau et al. 2017 Bayesian meta-analysis and Elkjær et al. 2022 power-posing meta-analysis were not retrieved; the felt-power (subjective) effect, which some meta-analyses report as surviving, is not documented here.
- No evidence on classroom "brain breaks" was gathered before the search budget ran out.
- Vohs et al. 2021's exact pooled d was not retrieved.

## Protocol sheet for a guided "Break" timer (what the evidence above supports)

### Takeaway
Three protocols are implementable from the evidence with honest grades: resonance breathing 5 min (A/B), a 5-10 min walk or stair climb (B), and a 10-min nap where conditions allow (B/C); cyclic sighing, box breathing before a stressor, 40-s green views, brief mindfulness, PMR, cold face immersion and music are optional with grades B-C and explicit caveats.

### Cited Findings
- Resonance breathing: ~6 breaths/min (resonance band 4.5-6.5 bpm), minimum 5 min, seated, not standing, uninterrupted — [Lehrer 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341254440_Heart_Rate_Variability_Biofeedback_Improves_Emotional_and_Physical_Health_and_Performance_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta_Analysis); [Bentley 2023](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Breathing-Practices-for-Stress-and-Anxiety-of-Based-Bentley-D%E2%80%99Andrea-Penna/b9ed440871b48c903451b4807b4a5c475cd643cc); [Laborde 2022](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/effects-of-voluntary-slow-breathing-on-heart-rate-and-heart-rate-/)
- Cyclic sighing: 5 min/day, inhale-emphasis 1:2 inhale:exhale, double nasal inhale then long mouth exhale — [Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Cyclic_Sighing)
- Box breathing: 4-4-4-4 pattern used as pre-stressor buffer, n=66 RCT — [PMC13321280](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13321280/)
- Movement: 5-min moderate walk hourly (n=30 crossover) or 10-min easy stair walking (small RCT) for vigor; ≥11 min for cognition — [PMC5094084](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5094084/); [UGA news](https://news.uga.edu/stairs-more-energy-research/); [PMC8992970](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8992970/)
- Nap: 10 min, not 20-30 min — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16796222/)
- Break content: passive rest beats task-switching for vigilance — [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf)
- Break length: micro-breaks ≤10 min give d ≈ 0.35 on vigor/fatigue; longer is better for performance — [PMC9432722](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)

### Inferences
- Suggested timer defaults: (1) "Reset" = 5:00 paced breathing at 6 bpm with a 4-s inhale / 6-s exhale visual cue; (2) "Energise" = 5:00-10:00 walk/stairs with a return cue; (3) "Pre-pitch" = 2:00-5:00 box breathing 4-4-4-4; (4) "Rest" = 5:00-10:00 eyes-off-screen rest, optional window/green view or self-chosen music; (5) "Nap" = 10:00 sleep opportunity, conditional on a quiet space. Label (1) and (2) as best-supported, (3) as single-RCT, (4) as mood-not-attention, (5) as conditional.
- Avoid: 30-60 s breathing claims (below the 5-min threshold flagged in Bentley 2023), 90-min cycle framing, binaural beats, power poses, and any promise that micro-breaks raise creative output.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests any of these protocols specifically in creative-agency or design workers; all transfers are from students, office workers, police or clinical samples.
